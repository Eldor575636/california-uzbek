How conspiracy theories spread and how to recognize them

What a conspiracy theory is, how it differs from a real conspiracy, the recurring features that help you recognize one, why people believe them and how social media amplifies them — a media-literacy guide based on verified, academic and international sources.

24 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

Conspiracy theories are a common feature of the news and of social media. This guide explains what such a theory is, how it differs from a real conspiracy, which features help identify one, why people believe them, and how platforms spread them — relying only on verified, academic and international sources. The article neither endorses nor debunks any specific political claim; it only describes the method and the mechanism (Source: European Commission/UNESCO — 'Identifying conspiracy theories').

What a conspiracy theory is

According to a definition the European Commission and UNESCO produced with academics, a conspiracy theory is 'the belief that certain events or situations are secretly manipulated behind the scenes by powerful forces with negative intent.' Real conspiracies do occur, but they usually centre on a single, self-contained event and are exposed by journalists or whistle-blowers using documents and evidence. A conspiracy theory, by contrast, typically imagines a vast, almost all-controlling secret group and treats the absence of evidence as 'proof of the cover-up' — so no evidence can ever refute it. As the philosopher Karl Popper noted, a theory that cannot be tested by any evidence is not a scientific theory (Source: European Commission/UNESCO; Michael Butter, 'The Nature of Conspiracy Theories', Polity, 2020).

How to recognize one: recurring features

Researchers Stephan Lewandowsky and John Cook ('The Conspiracy Theory Handbook', 2020) identify seven traits of conspiratorial thinking: (1) contradictory — holding mutually contradictory ideas at once; (2) overriding suspicion of the official account; (3) nefarious intent — the 'conspirators' are assumed to have evil motives; (4) 'something must be wrong' — even when specific claims collapse, the belief that 'something is hidden' persists; (5) feeling both a persecuted victim and a hero; (6) immune to evidence — contrary evidence is reinterpreted as 'part of the conspiracy'; (7) re-interpreting randomness — seeing meaning and intent in coincidences. The European Commission/UNESCO guide lists six common elements: an alleged secret plot, a group of conspirators, something posing as 'evidence', the false assumption that nothing happens by accident, dividing the world into 'good and bad', and scapegoating particular groups (Source: Lewandowsky & Cook — The Conspiracy Theory Handbook; European Commission/UNESCO).

Why people believe them

According to psychologist Karen Douglas (University of Kent) and the American Psychological Association (APA), belief in conspiracy theories is often linked to three unmet needs: a need for knowledge and certainty (anxiety and uncertainty push people toward simple, firm explanations); a need for control and security (people who feel powerless or threatened are more drawn to such ideas); and a need for belonging and self-esteem (feeling part of a special group that 'knows the truth'). Two cognitive tendencies add to this: the mind is prone to see pattern and intent in randomness, and a 'proportionality' bias that assumes a big event must have a big cause (Source: APA — 'Why people believe in conspiracy theories'; APA — 'The Conspiratorial Mind', a meta-analysis of 279 studies).

How social media amplifies them

Algorithms that maximize engagement tend to amplify content that is prestigious, in-group, moral and emotional. Misinformation often exploits exactly that — it provokes outrage, which is shared more, and this in turn 'trains' both the algorithms and users to surface still more of the same. As a result, an unchecked claim can spread faster and further than the truth (Source: W. Brady et al. — The Conversation; 'Science' — 'Misinformation exploits outrage to spread online').

Historical examples (already debunked)

'The Protocols of the Elders of Zion' is a forged document that appeared around 1903 in the Russian Empire, purporting to 'reveal' a Jewish plan for world domination. In 1921 the London Times proved that much of it was plagiarized from Maurice Joly's 1864 satire (which never mentions Jews); it is attributed to the Tsarist secret police (Okhrana), and in 1964 a US Senate subcommittee called it 'a vicious hoax'. The supposed 'mass panic' caused by the 1938 'War of the Worlds' broadcast has been revised by historians (Pooley and Socolow, 2013): the panic was in fact tiny and was exaggerated by newspapers competing with radio. Claims that the Moon landing was 'faked' have been refuted by much independent evidence (laser reflectors left by Apollo, lunar samples that cannot be produced on Earth, and Apollo hardware imaged by other nations' orbiters — India's and Japan's). The claim that 5G spreads COVID-19 is also baseless: radio waves cannot carry a virus, and the disease spread in countries without 5G (Source: US Holocaust Memorial Museum; Poynter; Wikipedia — 'Third-party evidence for Apollo Moon landings'; Ofcom).

How to check

A recommended media-literacy method is SIFT (the 'four moves', Mike Caulfield): Stop; Investigate the source; Find better coverage; Trace claims, quotes and images back to the original. Combine it with 'lateral reading': instead of staying on the page, open new tabs and check who is behind the claim and whether other reputable sources corroborate it. The European Commission recommends three checks: the author's credentials, the source's reliability, and the tone — genuine reporting acknowledges complexity and uncertainty, while a conspiracy theory presents a single, absolute 'truth'. Also ask: what evidence could disprove this claim? Who benefits? Is there a consensus among experts? (Source: Mike Caulfield — SIFT; Stanford History Education Group — lateral reading; European Commission).

How to talk to a believer

According to UNESCO's 'Think Before Sharing' (#ThinkBeforeSharing) guidance, when talking with a loved one who believes a conspiracy theory it is best to show empathy and avoid ridicule and open argument — because an aggressive debate can backfire, with any counter-argument absorbed as 'proof' that 'you too are part of the conspiracy'. Keep the relationship, speak respectfully; and online, sharing a link to a reliable fact-checking source is more effective than a drawn-out argument (Source: UNESCO — 'Addressing conspiracy theories'; the #ThinkBeforeSharing campaign).

Why this matters

Knowing the structure of conspiracy theories and how they spread helps you read the news critically: it shows which claims cannot be tested, why emotional content spreads faster, and how practical verification methods work. This article presents only verified information and historical examples; it evaluates no current political claim.
