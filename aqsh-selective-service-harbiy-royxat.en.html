Selective Service registration in the US: a guide for immigrant families

Almost all men aged 18–25 living in the US — citizens, green-card holders, refugees, asylees, and many undocumented men too — must register with the Selective Service within 30 days of turning 18. There is currently no active draft — this is only a standby list. Who must register, who is exempt, how to register, and the consequences of not registering. Based on sss.gov and USCIS — not legal advice.

25 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

Almost all men living in the US are required by law to join a military registry called the Selective Service at a certain age. Many immigrant families do not know this and later run into trouble with citizenship or other benefits. Below the process is set out with facts, based on the official site of the Selective Service System (SSS), sss.gov, and the rules of USCIS (the US Citizenship and Immigration Services). This is a general reference, not legal advice; for an individual case, consult a qualified immigration attorney or the official sites.

What the Selective Service is

The Selective Service System is an independent US federal agency. It maintains a standby registry so that, in an emergency and only if Congress and the President decide, young men can be mobilized into the armed forces. It does not itself conscript anyone and does not run a draft — it only keeps the list. The core rule is short: “Almost all male US citizens and male immigrants, who are 18 through 25, are required to register with Selective Service” (Source: sss.gov — Who Needs to Register).

Who must register — immigrants too

The duty to register is not tied to citizenship. Men aged 18–25 — US citizens, lawful permanent residents (green-card holders), refugees, asylees, parolees, and even many undocumented men — must register. Under USCIS rules, men generally must register within 30 days of their 18th birthday; if a person arrives in the US or becomes a green-card holder between ages 18 and 26, he registers within 30 days of arriving or of getting the card (Source: USCIS — Policy Manual, Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 7; sss.gov). An important point: the Selective Service does not ask about immigration status when registering and has no authority to do so — registering does not increase the risk of deportation (Source: sss.gov).

Who is exempt — women and some temporary visas

Women in the US have never been required to register — the law applies only to men. Also exempt are men who remained in the US in a fully lawful “nonimmigrant” (temporary visa) status for the entire 18–26 period: for example, a holder of an F-1 (student), H-1B (worker), tourist, or diplomatic visa, while the visa is valid, need not register (Source: USCIS — Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 7; sss.gov). However, a man who loses that status or overstays his visa must register if he is still under 26. Men over 26, and those who did not live in the US during ages 18–26, are also exempt (Source: USCIS).

There is no active draft right now

Registering does not mean being called up for military service. There is currently no active draft in the US — the military is entirely all-volunteer. The last draft was during the Vietnam War, and the draft ended in 1973. Mandatory registration was reinstated in 1980 and has remained in place since, but it is only a standby measure — no one is called up unless Congress passes a separate law (Source: Congressional Research Service — The Selective Service System and Draft Registration; sss.gov).

How to register

There are several ways to register. The easiest is online at the official site sss.gov. You can also register with a card at the post office, or by checking the registration box when filing the FAFSA for federal student aid. In many states, getting a driver's license or state ID registers you automatically — according to the SSS, 44 states and territories have linked their DMV to the Selective Service (Source: sss.gov; Congressional Research Service).

Age 26 — the most important deadline

Age 26 is a hard cutoff. The Selective Service accepts registration only up to one's 26th birthday; after that, the window closes permanently. That is why a man aged 18–25 should register without delay. If a person fails to register between ages 18 and 26 and cannot then correct it, he may be permanently barred from some federal benefits — an exception can be granted only if he can prove the failure to register was not “knowing and willful” (Source: sss.gov — Men 26 and Older; USCIS).

Consequences of not registering

Failing to register can cost several benefits. According to the SSS, a man who did not register may be denied: US citizenship (naturalization) for immigrant men, most federal jobs, federal job training under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act, and state-funded student aid and some state jobs in 31 states. By law, failing to register is a felony punishable by a fine of up to $250,000 and/or up to 5 years in prison (though in practice no one has been prosecuted for it since the 1980s) (Source: sss.gov — Benefits & Penalties). Note: since 2021, federal student aid (FAFSA) is no longer tied to registration — the FAFSA Simplification Act removed that requirement (Source: sss.gov; studentaid.gov).

A practical note for Uzbek families

Many Uzbek young men do not know this: a son with a green card, refugee, or asylee status who turns 18, or who moves to the US between ages 18 and 26, must register within 30 days. A young man staying temporarily on a student (F-1) or worker (H-1B) visa is exempt, but if he later gets a green card or loses his status — while still under 26 — he must register. USCIS checks this at the naturalization interview: a knowing, willful failure to register between ages 18 and 26 can hurt a citizenship application, while after age 26, since registration is no longer possible, the applicant is asked to prove his earlier situation (Source: USCIS — Policy Manual Vol. 12, Part D, Ch. 7).

An important note

Rules and the list of benefits change periodically (for example, the FAFSA requirement was removed in 2021). To clarify your own situation, verify the latest information at the official sss.gov and uscis.gov sites or with a qualified immigration attorney. This article is to explain the facts; it is not a substitute for legal advice on an individual case.
