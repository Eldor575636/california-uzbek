Greek political refugees in Tashkent: after the Greek Civil War (1949)

In 1949, after the defeat in the Greek Civil War, the Soviet Union took in thousands of Greek political refugees — the largest group was settled in Tashkent. The formation, life and 1980s repatriation of this community, based on verified facts.

24 November 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

In 1949, after the defeat of the communist side in the Greek Civil War, thousands of Greek political refugees were dispersed across the Eastern Bloc. In the Soviet Union the largest share was settled in Tashkent, in the Uzbek SSR. This article tells, with verified facts, how this community formed, how it lived, and how it eventually returned home (Source: Wikipedia — 'Greeks in Uzbekistan'; 'Refugees of the Greek Civil War').

The Greek Civil War and the defeat

The Greek Civil War (1946–1949) was fought between the Greek government forces and the Democratic Army of Greece (DSE), led by the Communist Party of Greece (KKE). The DSE had been founded on 28 October 1946. The decisive defeat came in August 1949 at the Battle of Grammos. By then more than 100,000 people — fighters and civilians — had left for Yugoslavia and the Eastern Bloc; some of the children were placed in Romania, Czechoslovakia, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria (Source: Wikipedia — 'Refugees of the Greek Civil War'; 'Democratic Army of Greece').

Why Tashkent

According to the historical record, roughly 11,900 Greek fighters were evacuated to the Soviet Union (about 8,573 men and 3,407 women) and directed mainly to Tashkent and Alma-Ata. The largest share — around 11,000 people — was settled in Tashkent, making the city the biggest centre of Greek political refugees in the Soviet Union. The KKE leadership, including party General Secretary Nikos Zachariadis, retreated to Tashkent in 1949. The main arrival was in the autumn of 1949, and the community consolidated in the early 1950s (Source: Wikipedia — 'Greeks in Uzbekistan'; 'Nikos Zachariadis'; cosmosphilly.com — 'Greeks of Tashkent').

Life in Tashkent

The refugees lived in close-knit communities. Greek children were taught Greek language, history and literature in school; the Association of Political Refugees, founded in 1964, had its own building housing offices and a school. Adults worked in textile factories, construction and agriculture; the community kept a cultural life with theatre, dance and music ensembles. In 1954–1956 political conflicts within the KKE (between Zachariadis loyalists and reformists) intensified and Soviet militia intervened. After the 1966 Tashkent earthquake, when the whole Union helped rebuild the city, Greek builders are widely reported to have taken part (Source: cosmosphilly.com; Greek American Experience — 'The Forgotten Greeks of Uzbekistan'; Wikipedia — '1966 Tashkent earthquake').

Population

The Greek community was at its largest in the 1960s–1970s: by various accounts, roughly 35,000 to 40,000 (a figure that includes Greeks who had lived in Uzbekistan before the war). The 1989 Soviet census recorded 10,453 Greeks in Uzbekistan. Alongside Koreans, Crimean Tatars, Germans and others, this group became part of Uzbekistan's multi-ethnic makeup (Source: Wikipedia — 'Greeks in Uzbekistan'; cosmosphilly.com).

Repatriation

After the fall of the military junta in Greece in 1974, and as the political climate later eased, the path home opened. In 1982 the PASOK government of Andreas Papandreou passed an amnesty law allowing Civil War political refugees to return and have their citizenship restored; this right, however, was limited to ethnic Greeks ('Greeks by genus'), excluding ethnic Macedonians. A later law allowed the reclaiming of confiscated property. Most of the refugees then returned to Greece during the 1980s (Source: Wikipedia — 'Refugees of the Greek Civil War'; Human Rights Watch report on Greek citizenship).

Today

Today the Greek population in Uzbekistan is much smaller — about 9,000 ethnic Greeks remain, and the community of political-refugee descendants in Tashkent is only about 1,200–1,500 people. The Greek Cultural Association of Tashkent runs Greek-language classes and preserves the community's cultural heritage (Source: Wikipedia — 'Greeks in Uzbekistan'; cosmosphilly.com).

Why this matters

The history of the Greek community in Tashkent shows how 20th-century geopolitics brought a people across a continent to another city for half a century, and it is one of the lesser-known pages of Uzbekistan's multi-ethnic history. This article presents only verified dates and figures; it offers no judgments or interpretation.
