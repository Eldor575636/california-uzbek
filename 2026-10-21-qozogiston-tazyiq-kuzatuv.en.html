Why sexual-harassment cases are hard to track in Kazakhstan

An incident on an Astana bus again highlighted the legal gap in recording and tracking public sexual harassment in Kazakhstan. With no dedicated criminal provision, such cases are not separated out in the statistics.

21 October 2026 · 2 min read · California Uzbek

An incident in Kazakhstan's capital, Astana, has revived debate about the legal gap in recording and tracking sexual harassment in public places. According to the Times of Central Asia, a woman who was harassed on a bus did not immediately go to the police, because she feared she could not prove what happened and had little confidence in law enforcement.

The Astana incident

The woman confronted the man, photographed him and posted about the incident on social media. Police later identified a 46-year-old man based on her post, and a court sentenced him to 10 days of administrative detention for indecent behaviour on public transport. The incident was also confirmed by the Kazakh outlets zakon.kz and nur.kz.

Why such cases are hard to track

Kazakhstan has no dedicated provision defining public sexual harassment of adults as a specific crime. Such cases are usually handled under Article 434 of the Code of Administrative Offenses — 'petty hooliganism' — which lumps harassment together with general public-order violations, so it cannot be separated out in the statistics. Article 123 of the Criminal Code covers only coercion into sexual acts through threats or blackmail, not unwanted touching or verbal harassment.

The figures and their limits

In a 2020 online survey by CABAR.asia (about 400 Central Asian respondents), 81% of women said they had experienced sexual harassment in taxis, while only 4.3% had reported it to the police. The authors stress that respondents were mostly young and urban, so the figures do not represent the whole population. According to the Times of Central Asia, a 2021 MediaNet survey found 82% of respondents considered sexual harassment a problem in Kazakhstan. Importantly, there is no reliable official figure for how many women are harassed on public transport each year — that statistical gap is the core of the problem.

Penalties and legal change

Under Article 434, the penalty can be a fine of about 20 monthly calculation indices (roughly 86,500 tenge, or about $190), 20–60 hours of community service, or 5–15 days of administrative detention. A bill to introduce liability for workplace harassment is also under discussion in Kazakhstan; because sources describe its exact stage differently, we do not present it as a settled fact.
