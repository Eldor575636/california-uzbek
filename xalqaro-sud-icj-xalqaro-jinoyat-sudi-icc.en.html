The International Court of Justice (ICJ) and the International Criminal Court (ICC): the difference between the two courts

The news often mentions 'the court in The Hague', but in fact two completely different courts sit in the Dutch city of The Hague: the International Court of Justice (ICJ) hears disputes between states, while the International Criminal Court (ICC) tries individuals for serious crimes. This explainer sets out the structure, jurisdiction and differences of the two courts based only on official and reliable sources.

28 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

In international news you will often meet phrases like 'the court in The Hague accused a state' or 'The Hague issued a warrant'. Many people assume this is one institution, but two entirely separate courts sit in the Dutch city of The Hague, and they differ fundamentally: the International Court of Justice (ICJ) and the International Criminal Court (ICC). Neither is the same building, nor do they do the same job. Below, the structure, jurisdiction and key difference of the two courts are set out based only on official documents and reliable sources (Source: ICJ official site; ICC official site; Britannica).

The International Court of Justice (ICJ)

The International Court of Justice was established in 1945 under the UN Charter and began work in 1946; its Statute is an integral part of the UN Charter. This court is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations and sits not in New York but in the Peace Palace in The Hague — it is the only principal UN organ whose seat is outside New York. The court has 15 judges, elected for nine-year terms by the UN General Assembly and the Security Council voting separately. Only STATES may be parties before the ICJ — not individuals, companies or organizations. The court performs two functions: it settles legal disputes between states and gives advisory opinions at the request of UN organs. Its judgments are binding on the parties and final, but the court has no enforcement arm of its own: enforcement runs through the UN Security Council, by political means (Source: ICJ official site; Britannica; Wikipedia — International Court of Justice).

The International Criminal Court (ICC)

The International Criminal Court is a much younger institution: its founding treaty, the Rome Statute, was adopted on 17 July 1998 and entered into force on 1 July 2002. This court is also located in The Hague, but not in the Peace Palace — in a separate, purpose-built premises. The ICC's most important difference from the ICJ is that it tries not STATES but INDIVIDUALS, for four serious crimes: genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression. The court works on the principle of 'complementarity' — it steps in only where national courts are unable or unwilling to handle a case; the national court takes priority. The ICC has 18 judges, and the Office of the Prosecutor conducts independent investigations and brings charges. As of 2024 the court has about 124–125 States Parties (members), with sources differing slightly on the exact number. An important point: the ICC is NOT a UN organ; it is an independent organization created by an international treaty, although it cooperates with the UN and the Security Council can refer situations to it (Source: ICC official site; Rome Statute — UN; ICC Assembly of States Parties).

The key differences

The easiest way to tell the two courts apart is to remember whom they judge, and for what. The ICJ hears disputes BETWEEN STATES (for example claims over borders, treaties or breaches of a convention) and gives legal advice; the ICC holds INDIVIDUALS criminally responsible. The ICJ is the UN's judicial organ, created by the UN Charter (1945); the ICC is an independent treaty body created by the Rome Statute (1998, in force 2002). The ICJ has 15 judges, the ICC has 18. Both sit in The Hague, but in separate buildings. An ICJ judgment is binding on a state, but its enforcement depends on the Security Council; the ICC convicts and sentences individuals, but relies on state cooperation to arrest and surrender them (Source: ICJ official site; ICC official site; Britannica).

Common confusions

These two courts are also widely confused with other international courts. The European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) is an entirely different institution: it sits in Strasbourg, belongs to the Council of Europe (46 member states) and hears human-rights complaints, including from individuals. The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) sits in Luxembourg and only interprets EU law. In short, with every news item it is worth asking 'which court?' — the ICJ or ICC in The Hague, or a completely different court in Strasbourg or Luxembourg (Source: Britannica; Wikipedia — European Court of Human Rights; Court of Justice of the European Union).

Uzbekistan and these courts

Uzbekistan became a UN member on 2 March 1992 and is therefore, under Article 93 of the UN Charter, automatically a party to the Statute of the International Court of Justice (ICJ) — all UN members are parties to the ICJ Statute. With the International Criminal Court (ICC) the situation is different: Uzbekistan SIGNED the Rome Statute on 29 December 2000 but has NOT RATIFIED it, so it is not a State Party to the ICC and has no treaty obligation to cooperate with the court. In Central Asia, ICC membership is as follows: Tajikistan was the only state to have ratified the Rome Statute, in 2000 (note: in November 2026 Tajikistan gave notice of withdrawal, but under Article 127 of the Rome Statute that decision takes effect one year later); Kyrgyzstan has signed but not ratified; Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan have not signed at all (Source: UN Charter, Article 93; Coalition for the ICC; Parliamentarians for Global Action — Rome Statute status).

Why this matters

To understand international news correctly you need to distinguish which court is being discussed: when a state brings another state to the ICJ it is an inter-state dispute; when the ICC issues a warrant against a person it is a criminal charge against a specific individual. The two cannot stand in for each other. The specific cases and warrants mentioned in this article (such as claims between states or warrants against certain leaders) are given only as examples; the explainer itself focuses on the difference in structure and jurisdiction, which does not change over time. All figures and dates are given based only on official and reliable sources; where sources differed (for example on the number of States Parties) the information is given in cautious form.
