How Islamic law is derived: the Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma' and qiyas (the basics of usul al-fiqh)

The answer to 'why is this halal, why is that haram?' is not a random opinion — it is derived through a specific methodology. This explainer distinguishes the terms sharia, fiqh and usul al-fiqh, and plainly explains the four main sources from which rulings are derived in Sunni Islam — the Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma' and qiyas. The article is mainstream Sunni (mainly Hanafi), neutral, and based only on reliable sources.

28 November 2026 · 6 min read · California Uzbek

Most people hear that an act is halal or haram in Islam, but rarely consider where that ruling comes from, or by what rule it is derived. In fact fiqh rulings are not random opinions — they are derived from agreed sources through a precise methodology called usul al-fiqh. This explainer first distinguishes three concepts — sharia, fiqh and usul al-fiqh — and then explains, in order, the four main sources in Sunni Islam: the Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma' and qiyas. The article does not enter into inter-school polemics; it is only about the sources and the method, written neutrally and based on reliable sources (Source: Oxford Reference — Fiqh; Egypt's Dar al-Ifta — Islamic Legal Theory).

Sharia, fiqh and usul al-fiqh — the difference

These three words are often used interchangeably in everyday speech, but scholars distinguish them carefully. SHARIA (Arabic for 'path') is the divine guidance shown by God in the Qur'an and the example of the Prophet (peace be upon him); as the divine ideal it is regarded as unchanging and complete. FIQH (literally 'deep understanding') is the HUMAN effort of qualified scholars to turn that divine path into concrete rulings through study, reflection and debate; because fiqh is a human product it can err, change and give rise to legitimate differences. USUL AL-FIQH (literally 'the roots/principles of fiqh') is the methodology for deriving fiqh from revelation — the theory that determines which sources and rules of interpretation to rely on. In short: sharia is the divine path; fiqh is scholars' human understanding of it; usul al-fiqh is the disciplined method by which that understanding is derived. This is precisely why sincere scholars can reach different conclusions without any of them 'not knowing God's law' (Source: Oxford Reference — Fiqh; Egypt's Dar al-Ifta; IslamQA — the difference between Sharia, Fiqh and Usul al-Fiqh).

The four main sources

The four Sunni schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali) agree on four sources for deriving rulings, in order of authority. (Some books call all four 'primary'; others separate the Qur'an and Sunnah as the two primary/revealed sources and ijma' and qiyas as the two secondary/derived sources — both framings are common.) The first is the QUR'AN: the word of God revealed to the Prophet (peace be upon him) over about 23 years; it is the first and highest source, and no ruling may contradict it. It should be noted that the Qur'an is first of all a book of guidance, and of its more than six thousand verses only a few hundred deal directly with legal rulings — which is exactly why the other sources serve to elaborate its principles (Source: Egypt's Dar al-Ifta; Oxford Reference).

The second source is the SUNNAH (or hadith): the sayings, actions and tacit approvals of the Prophet (peace be upon him), transmitted through reports called hadith. The Sunnah explains, specifies and details the Qur'an — for example, the Qur'an commands prayer, but the number of units and how to perform it come from the Sunnah. Only authentic (sahih) hadith carry legal weight; this is why hadith scholarship developed a rigorous method of verifying the chain of transmission (isnad) and the reliability of narrators. The third source is IJMA': the unanimous agreement of the qualified scholars (mujtahids) of a given era on a point of law. Its authority rests on the principle that the community of the ummah's scholars will not unite upon an error; once a genuine consensus is reached on an issue, it is treated as binding. In practice the most secure consensus belongs to the earliest generations, and how later consensus can be established is debated among scholars (Source: Wikipedia — Ijma; Oxford Reference; Egypt's Dar al-Ifta).

The fourth source is QIYAS: extending a ruling established in the texts (the Qur'an or Sunnah) to a new case not mentioned in them, because they share the same 'illa (effective cause). Qiyas has four pillars: the asl (the original textual case), the far' (the new case), the ruling of the asl, and the 'illa (the cause that triggers the ruling). The classic textbook example: the Qur'an prohibits khamr — grape wine (Surah al-Ma'ida, verse 90). Scholars identified the 'illa of that prohibition as the property of intoxication (iskar). On the basis of that 'illa, the prohibition extends to all intoxicants sharing the same cause — including modern narcotics unknown in the age of revelation. That is, the ruling follows the cause: wherever there is intoxication, the prohibition applies (Source: Wikipedia — Qiyas; Egypt's Dar al-Ifta).

Supplementary tools (which vary by school)

Beyond the four main sources there are supplementary tools that some schools accept to varying degrees; their status and definition differ from school to school, so they are treated not as 'universally recognized sources' but as 'tools that some jurists use.' Istihsan — departing from strict analogy in favor of a ruling that better fits the aim of the law in a particular case; it is most associated with the Hanafi school. Istislah/maslaha — a ruling based on the general welfare where the texts are silent; it is mainly associated with the Maliki school. 'Urf — sound local custom that does not contradict the texts; it is widely applied in Hanafi (and Maliki) fiqh, especially in transactions and contracts — which is also significant for the Central Asian/Uzbek context. Istishab — presuming that a state of affairs continues as before until there is evidence that it has changed (for example the presumption of innocence). Sadd al-dhara'i — blocking an otherwise-permissible act when it reliably leads to harm or a forbidden outcome; it is most associated with the Maliki and Hanbali schools. It should be noted that scholars disagree about which of these count as an independent 'source' and which are a facet of qiyas/ijtihad, and about how much weight each carries — and that disagreement is itself a natural feature of the tradition (Source: Wikipedia — Istihsan; Principles of Islamic jurisprudence; Oxford Reference).

Ijtihad, taqlid, and why the schools differ

Ijtihad is a qualified scholar exerting his utmost effort to derive a ruling directly from the sources using usul al-fiqh; one who does this is a mujtahid, subject to demanding conditions such as mastery of Arabic, the Qur'an, hadith, the points of consensus and legal theory. The founders of the four schools — Abu Hanifa, Malik, al-Shafi'i and Ahmad ibn Hanbal — are regarded as mujtahids of the highest rank. Taqlid, by contrast, is a person who is not himself a mujtahid following a qualified scholar or school; in the mainstream view an ordinary Muslim follows qualified scholarship rather than attempting independent derivation — much as a layperson trusts a doctor. Although the four Sunni schools agree on the same four sources, the reason they sometimes reach different rulings is that they apply these sources and the supplementary tools with different weight (for example, how far a solitary hadith is accepted against a general principle or local practice, or how much room is given to istihsan or 'urf). The differences are mostly in method and detail, not in core belief (Source: Oxford Reference; Wikipedia — Principles of Islamic jurisprudence).

Who systematized usul al-fiqh

Legal reasoning existed in Islam from the start (Abu Hanifa died in 767, Malik in 795), but the scholar credited with shaping usul al-fiqh into a separate, systematic discipline is Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 CE / 150–204 AH). His treatise 'al-Risala' is described in reputable sources as among the earliest/first systematic works on usul al-fiqh — setting out the hierarchy of sources (Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma', qiyas) and the rules of deriving evidence. For accuracy, the careful phrasing is that al-Shafi'i played a formative role in the systematization of usul al-fiqh and is regarded as its founder, and that 'al-Risala' is among the earliest systematic treatises on the subject (Source: Wikipedia — Al-Shafi'i; Oxford Reference).

Why this matters for an ordinary reader

Knowing these foundations is useful in several ways. First, understanding where a ruling comes from: a fatwa or ruling is not a random opinion but the result of a systematic method applied to agreed sources — the chain Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma', qiyas clarifies the question 'why is this halal/haram?' Second, understanding why qualified scholars differ: because fiqh is the human understanding of divine sharia, difference between the schools is natural, ancient and respected. Third, media literacy: this knowledge teaches a person to ask the right questions about any 'fatwa' seen online — Who issued it? Are they a qualified scholar? On what source and evidence is it based? Which school? This guards against both random 'internet fatwas' and the misuse of religious-legal terms in political or extremist content. This article quotes no specific fatwa or hadith text; it focuses only on the general structure of the sources and method, and for specific rulings it is recommended to ask qualified scholars.
