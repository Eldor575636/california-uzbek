US family-based immigration: how to petition a relative (Form I-130)

Which relatives can a US citizen or a green-card holder sponsor? The difference between 'immediate relatives' and the 'family preference' categories (F1–F4), Form I-130, the Visa Bulletin and the waiting rules — a reference guide.

23 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

One of the most common paths in the US immigration system is sponsoring a relative through family. The first document is Form I-130, the 'Petition for Alien Relative,' filed with USCIS (U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services). It is filed by a US citizen or a lawful permanent resident (green-card holder, LPR) and serves to formally establish the family relationship between the petitioner and the relative (Source: USCIS — Form I-130).

'Immediate relatives': a category with no wait

A US citizen's 'immediate relatives' fall into three categories: spouse; unmarried child under 21; and parent — in this case the petitioning citizen must be 21 or older. The key point: immediate relatives are not subject to any annual numerical limit — a visa is always available for them and there is no separate wait for a number (Source: USCIS — Family of U.S. Citizens).

Family preference categories (F1–F4): capped and with a wait

Other relatives fall into the 'family preference' categories, which are subject to annual numerical limits and therefore wait years. F1: the unmarried adult (21+) son or daughter of a US citizen. F2A: the spouse and unmarried children under 21 of a lawful permanent resident (green-card holder). F2B: the unmarried adult (21+) son or daughter of a green-card holder. F3: the married son or daughter of a US citizen. F4: the brother or sister of a US citizen — here the petitioning citizen must be over 21. Family-sponsored preference immigration is allotted roughly 226,000 visas worldwide per year (plus separate per-country limits); this is precisely why these categories back up for years (Source: USCIS — Family of U.S. Citizens; USCIS — Family of Green Card Holders; Congressional Research Service, R43145).

Citizen vs green-card holder: the difference

Who may be sponsored depends on the petitioner's status. A green-card holder (LPR) may petition only for a spouse, unmarried children under 21 (F2A) and unmarried adult children (F2B); an LPR cannot petition for parents, siblings or married children. A US citizen may petition for all of those plus parents, married children (F3) and siblings (F4); in addition, a citizen's spouse, children under 21 and parents fall into the no-wait 'immediate relative' category. If a green-card holder naturalizes, their relatives typically move to a faster category (Source: USCIS — Family of Green Card Holders; USCIS — Family of U.S. Citizens).

The process steps

The general order is as follows. First the petitioner files Form I-130 with USCIS — the step that establishes only the family relationship. After USCIS approves it, the relative obtains a green card by one of two routes: if inside the US and eligible, through Form I-485 (adjustment of status) — immediate relatives can often file it together with the I-130, while preference cases must wait for a visa number; if abroad, through consular processing — the case goes to the National Visa Center (NVC), the relative files Form DS-260 (immigrant visa application) and documents, then attends an interview at a US embassy or consulate (Source: USCIS — Form I-130; USCIS — Family of Green Card Holders).

The Visa Bulletin and the 'priority date'

The Visa Bulletin is a document the US Department of State publishes each month, showing in which category and up to which priority date applicants may proceed. The 'priority date' is the date USCIS received the I-130 and marks the relative's place in line. Because the preference categories (F1–F4) share a limited overall quota, when demand exceeds supply a cutoff ('final action') date forms, and applicants wait — often years — until their priority date becomes current. Immediate relatives have no cap and therefore no such wait (Source: USCIS — Family of U.S. Citizens; State Department — Visa Bulletin).

Fee and important cautions

Under the fee schedule dated 1 April 2024, the I-130 fee is about $675 (paper) or $625 (online); fees can change, so verify the current amount at uscis.gov before filing. An important point: an approved I-130 does not by itself grant immigration status, a green card or work authorization — it only establishes the relationship, and the remaining steps are taken separately. Beware of fraud as well: in the US a notary public is not authorized to give legal advice, so use only a licensed attorney or a government-accredited representative (Source: USCIS — Form I-130; USCIS — G-1055 Fee Schedule; USCIS — Avoid Scams).

Why this matters

Family-based immigration is one of the most common paths for the Uzbek diaspora in the US. Knowing in advance which category you fall into and the waiting rules prevents unexpected delays and mistakes. This article is a general reference, not legal advice; for the details of a specific case, consult official USCIS sources or a licensed professional.
