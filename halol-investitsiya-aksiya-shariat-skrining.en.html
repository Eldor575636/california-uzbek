Halal investing: how stocks are screened for Shari'ah compliance

Why buying a share differs from a bond, how shares are vetted through a two-step Shari'ah screen (business activity and financial ratios), how the AAOIFI, Dow Jones Islamic Market and MSCI standards differ, and what dividend "purification" (tat-hir) means — a facts-only explainer.

10 November 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

For many Muslim investors the question is simple: is buying a stock (a share) halal? The short answer is that a share itself represents part-ownership of a company and is, unlike an interest-bearing bond, permissible in principle. But not every company qualifies. That is why scholars developed a two-step "screening" test. The following explains that system, facts only.

Why a share differs from a bond

A bond is debt: its holder earns a fixed interest coupon, which is riba. A share is ownership of part of a company: if it profits, you get dividends and price gains; if it loses, the value falls. That is genuine partnership and risk-sharing. For this reason the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the OIC ruled that trading shares is permissible — provided the company's core business is halal and several conditions are met.

Step one: the business screen

First, the company's core business is examined. Companies in the following sectors are excluded outright, because their main income is forbidden:

  • Conventional (interest-based) banking and insurance — they run on riba;
  • Production or sale of alcohol;
  • Pork and pork-derived products;
  • Gambling, casinos and betting;
  • Tobacco;
  • Adult (indecent) content and the forbidden parts of the entertainment industry.

If a company's core business is halal but a small share of income comes from a forbidden source (for example a bar inside a hotel), the rule is: non-permissible income must not exceed 5% of total revenue. Above that, the stock is deemed non-compliant.

Step two: the financial ratios

Even with a halal core business, the company is checked for how much interest-bearing debt it carries and how much interest-bearing assets it holds. The three most-used standards and their limits are:

  • AAOIFI (the Bahrain-based standards board for Islamic financial institutions, the strictest): interest-bearing debt below 30% of market capitalization; interest-bearing cash and securities also below 30%; impure income below 5%. Measured at the spot market price.
  • Dow Jones Islamic Market (DJIM): a 33% limit for each ratio, but measured against a 24-month trailing average market capitalization.
  • MSCI Islamic: a 33.33% limit, but measured against total assets (the balance sheet) rather than market price.

So the same company can be "halal" under one standard and "non-compliant" under another — the thresholds and the method of calculation differ. That is why an investor must know which standard is being applied.

Supervisory boards and indices

Major indices are overseen by an independent Shari'ah Supervisory Board — scholars of Islamic law and finance. For example, the Dow Jones Islamic Market Index was launched in 1999 in Bahrain and is run by S&P Dow Jones Indices; the list of companies is reviewed regularly. Today FTSE, MSCI and other providers also offer Islamic indices.

Purification (tat-hir): cleansing the income

Even a company that passes the screen may earn a small amount of impure income (for example interest on cash held at a bank). In that case the investor calculates the portion attributable to their own holding and gives it to charity without expecting reward — this is called "purification" (tat-hir). That money does not count as zakat or as rewarded charity; it is given only to cleanse the wealth of the forbidden admixture.

Screening does not make everything halal

Screening only determines whether the company itself is compliant. How you trade is a separate matter: most scholars forbid trading on interest-based margin, short-selling, and speculation involving excessive uncertainty (gharar). In other words, a halal stock can still be bought in a forbidden way — the two are distinct conditions.
