The U.S. court system and jury duty: the basics for immigrants

The U.S. has two parallel court systems: the federal courts (94 district courts → 13 courts of appeals → the Supreme Court) and each of the 50 states' own courts. A criminal case is brought by the government; a civil case is usually a private dispute. The Constitution guarantees the right to a jury. To serve on a federal jury you must be a U.S. CITIZEN: green-card or visa holders cannot serve. If you are summoned by mistake, do not ignore it — fill out the form and indicate you are not a citizen. A reference — facts only, not legal advice.

9 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

A jury summons raises questions for many immigrants: what is it, who serves, and what should you do if you are not a citizen? The following explains, facts only, the structure of the U.S. court system, the difference between civil and criminal cases, the right to a jury, and the most important rules for non-citizens. This reference is not legal advice.

Two court systems: federal and state

The federal courts and the 50 states' courts operate in parallel. The federal system rests on Article III of the Constitution: 94 federal district courts, where trials begin; above them 13 courts of appeals (12 regional circuits plus the Federal Circuit for specialized cases such as patents); and the U.S. Supreme Court at the top. Each state has its own courts: trial courts → often intermediate appellate courts → a state supreme court.

Which court hears which case

Federal courts hear constitutional questions, federal laws and treaties, disputes between states, bankruptcy, and large civil suits between citizens of different states. State courts hear most everyday matters — most criminal cases, family law, probate, and contract and tort cases; state courts are the final arbiters of their own state's laws.

Civil vs. criminal cases

In a criminal case the government prosecutes a person accused of breaking the law; the penalty can be a fine or imprisonment. A civil case is usually a dispute between private parties (people or organizations), often over money or rights. The standard of proof also differs: criminal cases require "beyond a reasonable doubt" — the highest standard; civil cases use "preponderance of the evidence," meaning the claim is "more likely than not" (just over 50%).

The right to a jury

The 6th Amendment guarantees a jury in criminal cases and the 7th Amendment in federal civil cases. There are two kinds of jury: a grand jury does not decide guilt — it only decides whether there is "probable cause" that a crime occurred and whether to indict (16–23 members at the federal level). A trial (petit) jury hears the evidence at trial and returns the verdict (guilty/not guilty).

Jury size and a unanimous verdict

A federal criminal trial jury is normally 12 people, and the verdict must be unanimous. A federal civil jury can be as few as 6. Some states use fewer than 12 jurors and in some cases allow a non-unanimous verdict — the rules vary from state to state.

The requirement to serve: being a U.S. citizen

To serve on a federal jury all of the following are required at once: being a U.S. citizen; being at least 18; having resided primarily in the district for at least one year; being able to read, write, understand and speak English adequately; having no mental/physical condition that bars service; not currently being charged with a crime punishable by more than one year; and (unless civil rights were restored) never having been convicted of a felony. The citizenship requirement is set in law at 28 U.S.C. § 1865.

Non-citizens cannot serve

Green-card or visa holders CANNOT serve as jurors, because they are not U.S. citizens. Even so, people are sometimes summoned by mistake: jury lists are often drawn from voter-registration and DMV (driver's-license) records, so a non-citizen's name can end up on the list (for example because they were unintentionally registered to vote at the DMV).

What to do if you are summoned by mistake

If you receive a summons in error, do NOT ignore it. The correct step is to complete the form or qualification questionnaire and indicate that you are NOT a U.S. citizen. Being disqualified does not excuse you from responding; penalties for failing to respond apply regardless of eligibility. The exact procedure (which box, mail or phone) varies by court — follow the instructions on your own summons.

The jury-duty process and employer protection

The process: a summons → completing and returning the qualification questionnaire → voir dire in court (selecting jurors by questioning). Under the Jury System Improvements Act of 1978 (28 U.S.C. § 1875) an employer may not fire, threaten or coerce a permanent employee because of federal jury service; violations bring damages and a civil penalty (up to $5,000 per employee). Federal jurors are paid $50 a day (up to $60 a day after 10 days if the judge so orders), plus travel and parking reimbursement.

An important caution

Immigration law treats a false claim to U.S. citizenship very seriously: under INA § 212(a)(6)(C)(ii) a non-citizen who falsely represents being a citizen for any purpose can be found inadmissible, and under INA § 237(a)(3)(D) can be found removable. Serving on a jury implies claiming to be a citizen. So a non-citizen should NOT serve — only indicate that they are not a citizen. This reference is not legal advice; if in doubt, consult an immigration attorney.
