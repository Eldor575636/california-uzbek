U.S. 529 education savings plans: tax-advantaged saving for your child's education

A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged way to save in the U.S. for a child's — or your own — education costs. The money grows tax-free, and if it is spent on education, even the growth is withdrawn tax-free. This guide explains the two types of 529 plan, the tax rules, what the money can be used for, the contribution limits and the key 2026 figures, based solely on official sources (IRS, SEC Investor.gov). This is general information, not personal tax or financial advice.

22 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

For Uzbek families in the U.S., paying for a child's education is one of the biggest expenses, and college tuition keeps rising year after year. That is why the U.S. tax system offers a dedicated, tax-advantaged way to save for education — it is called a «529 plan» (named after Section 529 of the U.S. tax code). This guide explains how it works, its rules and the key figures as of 2026, based solely on official sources.

What a 529 plan is, and its two types

A 529 plan (officially a «qualified tuition program») is a savings account meant for education costs, established by a state or an educational institution. There are two types. The first is a savings (investment) plan: your money is invested and can rise or fall in value; it can be used at almost any eligible school nationwide. The second is a prepaid tuition plan: it lets you buy future tuition at today's prices, but it is usually limited to certain schools in a given state and comes with more conditions.

The main benefit — a tax break

Money is contributed to a 529 plan with after-tax dollars — the amount you put in is not deductible from federal income tax. In return there are two major benefits: the money in the account grows tax-free, and if it is spent on qualified education expenses, the growth (earnings) is also withdrawn tax-free. If the money is used for something other than education, only the earnings portion is subject to ordinary income tax plus an additional 10% penalty (never your own contributions). The 10% penalty is waived in cases such as the beneficiary receiving a scholarship, dying or becoming disabled, though tax on earnings still applies. (Source: IRS, Topic 313.)

What it can be used for

Qualified higher-education expenses include tuition and mandatory fees, room and board for students enrolled at least half-time, and books, supplies and a required computer. In addition: K-12 school tuition — up to $10,000 per year per beneficiary; this limit was raised to $20,000 per year for tax years after 31 December 2025 (under a law signed in July 2025). Expenses for apprenticeship programs registered with the U.S. Department of Labor also qualify. For student-loan repayment, up to $10,000 over a lifetime per beneficiary may be used. (Source: IRS, Topic 313.)

How much you can contribute

There is no fixed federal annual contribution limit, but gift-tax rules apply. In 2026 one person can give up to $19,000 per year per beneficiary (a married couple up to $38,000) without a gift-tax return. Under the «superfunding» rule you may front-load five years of exclusions at once — up to $95,000 per person, or $190,000 per couple, in 2026 (a special form, Form 709, is filed, even though no tax is due). Each state sets an overall aggregate cap per account — typically roughly $235,000 to over $600,000, varying by state.

State tax breaks vary

State-level tax treatment is separate from the federal one and varies sharply from state to state: some states give no break, most give a deduction, and a few give a tax credit. For example, New York gives an income-tax deduction of up to $5,000 per year (up to $10,000 for a married couple filing jointly) on contributions to its own plan. Indiana gives a 20% tax credit — up to $1,500 per year. An important point: in many states you must use your own state's plan to get the break; money contributed to another state's plan usually does not qualify for the deduction.

The account owner and the beneficiary

In a 529 plan the money is controlled by the account owner (a parent or whoever opened the plan) — not the beneficiary (the child the money is intended for). All decisions belong to the owner until the money is withdrawn. If the beneficiary does not go to school or money is left over, the owner can change the beneficiary to another member of that family (for example a second child or a sibling) with no tax consequences. (Source: IRS «529 Plans: Q&A».)

Effect on financial aid (FAFSA)

When federal student aid is calculated (FAFSA), a parent-owned 529 is counted as a parental asset and affects the aid calculation by at most 5.64% of its value — a relatively small impact. After the FAFSA simplification that began with the 2024–2025 school year, money withdrawn from a grandparent-owned 529 no longer counts as student income and has almost no effect on the federal FAFSA (some private colleges may count it differently on their own forms).

If the money is not spent on education, or is left over

If the money is not used for education, as noted above, only the earnings portion is taxed and carries the 10% penalty. Under a newer rule effective from 2024 (the SECURE 2.0 Act), part of any leftover balance may be rolled into a Roth IRA retirement account in the beneficiary's name: up to $35,000 over a lifetime, but with conditions — the 529 must have been open at least 15 years, contributions from the last 5 years are not eligible, the amount rolled each year cannot exceed that year's Roth IRA limit, and the beneficiary must have at least that much earned income.

Important note

This is general, fact-based information, not personal tax or financial advice. The rules and figures of 529 plans (tax limits, state breaks, annual amounts) change from time to time; the figures here apply as of 2026. Before making a decision for your own situation, it is advisable to consult a qualified tax adviser or financial professional and the official IRS page.
