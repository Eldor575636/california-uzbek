How news media make money, and how bias arises

"Follow the money": advertising, subscriptions, membership, state funding and syndication — the revenue sources of media outlets and how each creates a slant (bias) in story selection, explained facts-only. A media-literacy explainer.

10 November 2026 · 2 min read · California Uzbek

A key rule for reading the news well is "follow the money." Every media outlet earns revenue in some way, and that source of revenue shapes which stories it chooses and what it emphasizes. This slant (bias) does not mean deliberate falsehood; it often arises simply from economic incentives. The following explains, facts only, how media make money and how bias arises.

How media earn revenue

News outlets have several main revenue sources:

  • Advertising — display (banner), classified and video ads; for a long time this was the main income of newspapers and broadcast;
  • Subscriptions and paywalls — the reader pays directly for content (for example digital subscriptions);
  • Membership and donations — readers or foundations provide support (the nonprofit-journalism model);
  • Public or state funding — public broadcasters funded by taxes or a license fee;
  • Sponsorship and sponsored content — paid material presented in article form;
  • Licensing and syndication — wire agencies such as AP and Reuters sell their material to other outlets;
  • Events and conferences.

The balance among these is shifting sharply. According to the Pew Research Center, advertising revenue at U.S. newspapers fell from $48.7 billion in 2000 to $9.8 billion in 2022 — about 80%. The digital share of ad revenue rose from 17% in 2011 to 48% in 2022. That is why many outlets have been forced toward subscriptions.

Where bias comes from

The revenue source influences story selection. Researchers note several mechanisms:

  • Advertiser influence: if most revenue comes from advertising, an outlet may steer away from topics that upset advertisers. Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman called this factor part of the "propaganda model" in Manufacturing Consent (1988);
  • Audience and subscription influence: a subscription-based outlet may favor material that confirms its paying readers' views, because readers pay to read what they already agree with;
  • Ownership: the commercial or political interests of an outlet's owner can affect headlines and emphasis;
  • State control: a state-funded or state-controlled outlet tends to reflect the official viewpoint;
  • Source dependence: reliance on official sources (government, police, companies) shapes which side of an event is shown;
  • The attention economy: a model that runs on ads and clicks rewards emotional, sharp headlines, because they generate more views.

Different models, different incentives

The key point: no funding model is "neutral" in itself; each creates its own incentives. An advertising model rewards a broad audience and many clicks; a subscription model rewards a loyal, like-minded reader; a state model rewards a government-friendly slant; a donation model rewards alignment with a funder's goals. That is why two reports of the same event can differ markedly.

Practical takeaway

From a media-literacy standpoint, several steps help: find out who funds an outlet; distinguish news, opinion and paid (sponsored) material; read the same event from several outlets with different funding models; look at the text and the sources, not the headline. More on these methods is in our other media-literacy articles on this site.
