How US college admissions work: SAT/ACT, the Common App and the application process

Admission to a US undergraduate program is a step-by-step process: standardized tests (SAT or ACT), a single application (the Common App), an essay, letters of recommendation and application deadlines. This guide explains the process for immigrant and diaspora families — based only on verified facts, not as advice. Exact rules and deadlines differ by college and change every year, so always check the college's official page.

27 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

Admission to a US undergraduate (four-year) program is a multi-stage process that usually spans the final two years of high school. For diaspora families it differs sharply from a system built on a single entrance exam: here the test score is only one part, with the rest made up of grade history, an essay, letters of recommendation and an activities list. The process is explained below in order, based only on verified information. This article gives no advice and stresses that exact rules differ by college and change every year (Source: Wikipedia — SAT; Wikipedia — Common App).

The overall timeline

The admissions process usually begins in the 11th grade (junior year): students take the SAT or ACT, build extracurricular and leadership experience, draw up a list of colleges and line up teachers to write recommendations. In the 12th grade (senior year) the essays are finalized, applications are submitted in the fall, and decisions come in the spring. Regular decisions are often announced in late March to April, and an admitted student confirms their place at one college by paying a deposit by May 1 (the national reply deadline) (Source: CollegeData — Application deadlines).

Types of application deadline

There are several ways to apply. 'Early Decision' (ED) is binding: if you are admitted you must enroll at that college and withdraw your other applications; the deadline is usually around November 1, with a decision in mid-December. 'Early Action' (EA) is a non-binding early application: you apply early (around November 1), hear back in December–January, but can decide later. 'Regular Decision' (RD) is the standard deadline: usually submitted around January 1, with a decision in late March to April. Some large public universities use 'rolling admission' — there is no single deadline and applications are reviewed as they arrive (Source: CollegeData — Application deadlines; Wikipedia — College admissions in the United States).

Standardized tests: SAT and ACT

There are two main standardized tests. The SAT is run by the College Board; since March 2024 it is fully digital in the US, taken on the 'Bluebook' app, and adaptive — the difficulty of the second section depends on the first. The SAT total score runs from 400 to 1600, from two sections — 'Reading and Writing' and 'Math', each scored 200–800. The ACT is run by ACT; its composite score runs from 1 to 36. On the enhanced ACT the required core sections are English, math and reading; the science and writing sections are optional and do not count toward the composite score (Source: Wikipedia — SAT; ACT.org).

In recent years many colleges have adopted a 'test-optional' policy, meaning an applicant may choose whether to submit an SAT or ACT score; not submitting a score does not automatically count against the application. The practice spread widely during the 2020 pandemic. At the same time, a number of selective universities reinstated the testing requirement in 2024–2025. Policies differ by college and change every year, so the current requirement of a specific college must always be checked on its official page (Source: Wikipedia — College admissions in the United States).

A single application: the Common App

Most colleges let you send a single application to many of them through the 'Common Application' (Common App); over 1,000 colleges are members. The Common App includes: a personal statement — a piece of up to 650 words on one of several prompts, sent the same to every college; a list of extracurricular activities and work experience; letters of recommendation, usually from a school counselor and one or more teachers; and an official transcript and grade point average (GPA) sent by the school. There is also an alternative platform called 'Coalition for College' (now run through Scoir), covering about 150 colleges (Source: Wikipedia — Common App; Wikipedia — Coalition for College).

Types of higher-education institution

There are several kinds of institution. A 'community college' is a two-year college that grants an 'associate' degree, usually has open admission and is cheaper. From it you can transfer to a four-year university — the 'transfer' path, governed by agreements between colleges (articulation agreements). In California, for example, there is a path from a community college into the UC or CSU system. Four-year universities can be public (funded by the state) or private (Source: California State University — Transfer; Wikipedia — Community college).

In building a list of colleges, the terms 'safety', 'match' and 'reach' are often used. A 'safety' is a college where admission is very likely (your scores are above that college's range); a 'match' (or target) is one where your scores are near the college's average; a 'reach' is a selective college that is harder to get into. Advisers usually describe building a balanced list across all three (Source: Wikipedia — College admissions in the United States).

Cost and financial aid

The total cost of attendance varies sharply from college to college. Financial aid usually begins by filing the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) at studentaid.gov. This subject is covered in detail in a separate article, so it is only mentioned briefly here (Source: Federal Student Aid — studentaid.gov).

Domestic and international applicants

An important distinction: US citizens and green-card holders (lawful permanent residents) apply as 'domestic' applicants and are eligible for federal aid (Pell Grant, federal loans) through the FAFSA. International students (on an F-1 visa) usually must prove English proficiency through a test such as TOEFL or IELTS (the exact requirement is set not by the visa but by each college); once admitted, the college issues a Form I-20, used to apply for the F-1 visa. F-1 students are not eligible for federal financial aid (Source: Federal Student Aid — studentaid.gov; U.S. Department of State — Student Visa).

Another key point: public universities charge residents of their own state a much lower tuition (in-state tuition). Establishing state residency usually requires living in the state continuously (often about 12 months) before enrollment, plus the intent to stay; rules vary by state. Out-of-state and international students pay significantly higher tuition (Source: California State University — Residency).

Why this matters

Understanding the US college admissions process in advance helps diaspora families plan, avoid missing deadlines and widen a child's options. This article relies only on verified sources; it offers no advice or guarantee. Tests, deadlines, test-optional policies and residency rules change every year — for the most accurate information always turn to the college's official page, the College Board, ACT and studentaid.gov.
