The third wave of Uzbek migration: post-Soviet labor migration (1991–2010)

The economic labor migration that began after the USSR collapsed — mainly to Russia, then Kazakhstan and Korea. A neutral history with dates, estimated figures and sources.

20 September 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

The earlier waves of Uzbek emigration — 1917–1934 and 1940–1960 — were mainly political and forced. The third wave, which began after the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, is one of economic and labor migration. The following is based only on verified sources; all migration figures from this era are estimates, because much migration went unrecorded and sources differ.

Context: the economic crisis of the 1990s

The 1991 collapse of the Soviet Union turned all Central Asian republics into labor-sending countries. The economic crisis of the 1990s, scarce jobs and low wages pushed many citizens to seek work abroad. Scholars describe this post-1991 period as one of economic rather than political migration.

Main destinations and scale

Russia became the primary destination. Per UN data (mid-2020), about 57% of Uzbek migrants lived in Russia; some studies found roughly 75% of labor migrants worked in Russia and 12% in Kazakhstan. Kazakhstan became the second destination around 2000 as its economy recovered. South Korea grew later, through organized labor programs.

The number of registered foreign labor migrants in Russia rose about 11-fold in 1999–2008, from 211,000 to 2.4 million (all nationalities). The Central Asian share of migrant workers in Russia rose from 6.3% in 2000 to 54.8% in 2010. The International Organization for Migration (IOM) estimated that in this period more than a million Uzbeks worked abroad each year, mainly in Russia and Kazakhstan. All of these figures are estimates.

Drivers

Russia's economic boom of the 2000s — the expansion of construction, retail and services — created huge demand for labor. The main pull factors were the large wage gap, geographic proximity and visa-free travel within the CIS. Remittances sent from Russia to other CIS states rose about sevenfold in 1999–2004, from $0.5 billion to $3.5 billion (Central Bank of Russia data).

Remittances

Uzbekistan has consistently ranked among the world's most remittance-dependent economies. According to World Bank data, remittances made up a large share of GDP (roughly the mid-teens in percent in peak years). Exact annual percentages vary by source, because Uzbekistan revised its GDP calculation in 2018. For comparison, in 2013 the figure was about 49% of GDP for Tajikistan and 32% for Kyrgyzstan.

Policy and turning points

In 2002 Russia adopted its first labor-migration law and a strict citizenship law; work-permit registration and migration cards were introduced. The number of registered labor migrants rose from 175,000 in 2001 to 570,000 in 2006. Reforms in 2006 simplified some procedures. From 2007 the Federal Migration Service tightened enforcement, carrying out document checks.

The global financial crisis of 2008–2009 sharply cut labor demand in Russia and reduced remittances to Central Asia. Migration was largely seasonal and circular — most Uzbeks did not settle permanently in Russia but went and returned as seasonal workers.

Who left

Studies show migrants were mostly young men — roughly 80–90% male, most of them under 30. They came mainly from rural areas (about 59% rural residents, per 2018 data). The Fergana Valley — Uzbekistan's most densely populated and youthful region — is noted as a major labor-sending area.

A note on sources and accuracy

All migration counts from this era are estimates: because of unrecorded and circular migration, true figures differ across sources (for example, Russia's migration service, the IOM and academic estimates). Exact remittance-to-GDP percentages are source-dependent owing to the 2018 GDP revision. Dates have been checked against several authoritative sources.
