What is Tasawwuf (Sufism): its foundations and the Yasawiyya and Naqshbandiyya orders

Sufism is the inner, spiritual dimension of Islam: a path of drawing near to God by purifying the heart and remembering Him. In the history of Uzbek and Central Asian Islam two orders hold a special place — the Yasawiyya, founded by Khoja Ahmad Yasawi, and the Naqshbandiyya, associated with Baha-ud-Din Naqshband. This guide explains the foundations of Sufism, its key terms, and the history and founders of these two orders, respectfully and based solely on reputable sources (Encyclopaedia Iranica, UNESCO and others).

21 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

Sufism (Arabic tasawwuf) is the inner, spiritual or mystical dimension of Islam, aimed at knowing and loving God through purification of the heart and devotion. It is rooted in ihsan, one of the three dimensions of the religion; in the Hadith of Gabriel, ihsan is defined as "to worship God as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, [to know] that He sees you." Sufism's central aim is to train and purify the self (tazkiyat an-nafs). The mainstream orders operate within the Sharia. Below, its foundations and the two orders closest to Central Asia are set out solely on the basis of reputable sources.

Key terms

Sufism has several key terms. A murshid (also shaykh or pir) is a spiritual master and guide. A murid is a disciple, one who has entered the Sufi path. A silsila is the spiritual chain linking a shaykh through his teachers, ultimately back to the Prophet. A tariqa is the "path" — a Sufi order. Dhikr is the remembrance of God, repeating His name on the tongue or in the heart; it is the central Sufi practice, performed alongside the obligatory acts of worship. A khanqah is the lodge where Sufis gather, a spiritual centre.

The Yasawiyya order and Khoja Ahmad Yasawi

The Yasawiyya was the first major Sufi order to spread among Turkic peoples, associated with Khoja Ahmad Yasawi. UNESCO calls him "a distinguished Sufi master of the 12th century"; his life is usually dated c. 1093–1166. "Yasawi" means "of the city of Yasi"; he died in Yasi (Turkestan) in present-day southern Kazakhstan. His teacher was Yusuf Hamadani. One of Yasawi's most important contributions was composing religious-spiritual poetry in the Turkic language, which greatly helped the spread of Islam among Turkic peoples. The Diwan-i Hikmat ("Book of Wisdom") attributed to him is a collection of hikmat (short Sufi poems), regarded as the first major Sufi work in a Turkic language (the text was expanded in later centuries, hence "attributed to him").

The Yasawi Mausoleum — a UNESCO site

The Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmad Yasawi in Turkestan was built on the order of Timur (Amir Temur) around 1389–1405, replacing a smaller earlier tomb; it was left unfinished because of Timur's death in 1405. This huge building, of the khanqah type uniting a mausoleum and a mosque, has one of the largest domes in Central Asia; construction techniques tested here were later used in Samarkand. The mausoleum was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2003. UNESCO links it directly to "the diffusion of Islam in this region with the help of Sufi orders."

The Naqshbandiyya order and Baha-ud-Din Naqshband

The Naqshbandiyya takes its name in honour of Baha-ud-Din Naqshband of Bukhara. Encyclopaedia Iranica gives his precise dates: born in 1318, died on 2 March 1389. He was born in the hamlet of Qasr-i Hinduvan near Bukhara; after his death the village was renamed Qasr-i Arifan — his tomb and memorial complex stand there. The name "Naqshband" refers to "imprinting" the Name of God on the heart through constant, silent invocation.

Silent dhikr and "Heart with God, hands at work"

The hallmark of the Naqshbandiyya is silent dhikr (dhikr performed inwardly, without voice). Following the guidance of the earlier master Abd al-Khaliq Ghijduvani, Baha-ud-Din made silent dhikr the order's normative practice. Its famous principles are "Khalwat dar anjuman" (solitude within the crowd — being inwardly with God while outwardly active in life) and "Dil ba yar, dast ba kar" (the heart with the Beloved — God — and the hands at work). These principles present Sufism not as withdrawal from the world but as spiritual sincerity within work and society.

The Khwajagan chain

The roots of the Naqshbandiyya go back to the spiritual chain of the "Khwajagan" (the Masters) in Central Asia. This chain runs through Yusuf Hamadani (d. 1140) and Abd al-Khaliq Ghijduvani; Ghijduvani was among the masters who shaped the order's core principles. Baha-ud-Din Naqshband held a key place in this chain and gave the order its name. In later centuries the Naqshbandiyya spread first among Turkic peoples, then widely across the Ottoman Empire and India (the reformist Mujaddidi branch, associated with Ahmad Sirhindi).

Its significance in Central Asia today

Today both shrines — the Baha-ud-Din Naqshband complex near Bukhara (Qasr-i Arifan) and the Yasawi Mausoleum in Turkestan — remain important spiritual centres drawing many pilgrims. The Naqshband complex has grown into an active ensemble of mosques, a madrasa, courtyards and guest cells, and has been restored several times. These monuments are an inseparable part of the Islamic and spiritual heritage of the Uzbek and Central Asian peoples.

Important note

This is general, fact-based information, not a religious ruling or advocacy. For deeper and more precise knowledge of the history, terms and practices of Sufism and the orders, it is advisable to consult qualified religious scholars and reliable academic sources. Some dates (for example, Yasawi's death — 1166 or 1167) differ slightly between sources.
