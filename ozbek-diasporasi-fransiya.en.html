The Uzbek diaspora in France: a small, young community centered on Paris

The Uzbek community in France is small, relatively new and concentrated mainly around Paris. Because French statistics do not record ethnicity, the exact number of Uzbeks is not officially known. This article relies only on verified facts: from the 1929 Paris émigré press to today's restaurants and cultural organizations. No unverifiable figures or claims are presented.

27 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

The Uzbek community in France is far smaller than the large diasporas in Russia, Turkey or Kazakhstan — relatively recently formed and concentrated mainly in Paris and the surrounding Île-de-France region. One point must be stressed at the outset: France's republican statistical tradition does not record the ethnicity or nationality of residents, so the exact number of Uzbeks in France is not officially known. This article relies only on verified facts and presents no unverified population figure (Source: Novastan — Novastan France; Wikipedia — Uzbeks).

Historical roots: the 1929 Paris émigré press

The earliest documented trace connected with France is not a mass migration but a small political-intellectual émigré node. Mustafa Chokay, the leader of the Turkestan independence movement, founded the journal 'Yosh Turkiston' ('Young Turkestan') in Paris in December 1929; it was edited from Paris, printed in Berlin, and ran 117 issues until August 1939. This shows there was a Turkestani political-intellectual émigré node in Paris in the 1930s — but it is not evidence of a mass Uzbek settlement. The main postwar Turkestani émigré centers were Germany and Turkey, not France (Source: Shokay.kz — A fiery publicist; Wikipedia — Baymirza Hayit).

The modern wave: from the 2000s onward

The modern migration of Uzbeks to France began mainly in the 2000s–2010s and is tied largely to labor and partly to asylum-seeking. France is not a leading destination in this respect, as Russia, Turkey or Kazakhstan are: within the European Union the leading destinations for residence permits issued to Uzbek citizens were Poland, Latvia and the Czech Republic, with France holding a small share. Asylum claims from Uzbekistan to France also exist, but Uzbekistan is not among the country's most common sources of asylum seekers (Source: CABAR.asia — Migrants from Central Asia in the EU; CNDA — Ouzbékistan).

Caution is needed with exact figures: no reliable, publicly released official count of Uzbekistan-born residents or Uzbek citizens in France could be found. For that reason this article presents no specific population figure — the community is assumed to number at most a few thousand, but there is no official source confirming this. As the only Europe-wide comparison point, in 2019 the number of labor-based permits issued to Uzbekistan nationals across the whole EU was about three thousand, most of them in Poland, Latvia and the Czech Republic (Source: CABAR.asia — Migrants from Central Asia in the EU).

Where they are concentrated

The visible part of Uzbek and Central Asian life in France is mainly in the city of Paris and the Île-de-France region — through restaurants, cultural organizations and a student community. Because the community is small and dispersed, there is no reliable source to tie it to a specific district or town and claim Uzbeks live there densely; the article therefore describes the community in general terms as being 'around Paris' (Source: Novastan — Novastan France).

Community life: restaurants, media and culture

Today the most clearly documented sign of an Uzbek presence in France is cuisine. The restaurant 'Samarkand' opened on 15 April 2005 in the Marais district of Paris and was described as the first Uzbek restaurant in Europe (it closed around 2010). Today active Uzbek and Central Asian eateries in Paris include places such as 'Plov.fr' and 'Samarcande' — showing the presence of Uzbek food culture in the city (Source: Novastan — Novastan France).

In media and intellectual life Paris holds a special place. 'Novastan' — the only European outlet (in French, German and English) devoted to Central Asia — was founded in Bishkek in 2011 and registered as a French association in July 2018, with its headquarters in Paris. It was built in part by Central Asian (including Uzbek) students who studied in France — showing the presence of student and associative life in Paris (Source: Novastan — Novastan France).

In the cultural sphere the association 'La Timuride' is also noted; it was founded on 27 March 1988 in Paris and promotes the history and culture of the Timurids and of Samarkand through conferences, concerts and exhibitions. It must be stated clearly: this is a French cultural-scholarly society devoted to Timurid heritage, not an Uzbek migrant community organization; it should therefore be understood not as an organization of the Uzbek diaspora but as one expression of interest in Uzbek culture in France (Source: Novastan — La Timuride interview).

A comparison: why 'small but growing'

The Uzbek diaspora worldwide is concentrated mainly in Russia (estimates range from several hundred thousand to over two million), Turkey and Kazakhstan; there is also a sizeable community in the US (about 68,000 Uzbekistan-born according to US data). Against these large centers, France is a far smaller, peripheral destination. For that reason, describing the Uzbek community in France as 'small, new and forming around Paris' is the most accurate and verified conclusion (Source: Wikipedia — Uzbeks; Pew Research — Uzbek Americans).

Why this matters

Knowing the history of the Uzbek community in France helps us understand how widely branched the diaspora is and how each country's community has its own character. This article was written with deliberate caution: because the exact number of Uzbeks in France is not officially recorded, no unverified figure was presented, and no unverified claims tying the community to a particular district were included. Only facts confirmed by reliable sources — the press, restaurants and cultural organizations — were used as the basis.
