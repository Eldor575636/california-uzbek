The establishment of Uzbekistan–Kazakhstan relations (1991–1996): diplomacy between neighbours

How did Central Asia's two large states establish ties in the early years of independence? The Alma-Ata Protocol, the 1992 diplomatic relations, the 1994 Central Asian Union, and the Karimov–Nazarbayev era — with verified dates.

23 November 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan are two of Central Asia's largest states, and their diplomatic ties took shape in the first years of independence. Both became independent in 1991: Uzbekistan declared state independence on 31 August 1991, and Kazakhstan — the last of the Soviet republics to do so — on 16 December 1991. In this way the two neighbours emerged onto the international stage as new states at nearly the same time (Source: Wikipedia — 1991 Uzbek independence referendum; Wikipedia — On the State Independence of the Republic of Kazakhstan).

The Alma-Ata Protocol and the CIS (1991)

The first formal multilateral contact between the two states began within the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS). On 21 December 1991 the Alma-Ata Protocol, signed in Kazakhstan's capital Alma-Ata (Almaty), enlarged the CIS; among the 11 signatory states were Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan — the document was signed by Islam Karimov and Nursultan Nazarbayev (Source: Wikipedia — Alma-Ata Protocol; CVCE — Signatories of the Alma-Ata agreement, 1991).

Diplomatic relations: 1992

Formal diplomatic relations between Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan were established on 23 November 1992. This date appears in Kazakhstan's list of foreign relations; because some sources devoted to bilateral ties are brief, the date rests mainly on the official Kazakh-side list (Source: Wikipedia — Foreign relations of Kazakhstan).

Central Asian integration (1993–1994)

In the early years of independence Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan (together with Kyrgyzstan) pursued economic integration. In 1993–1994 the three states signed agreements to create a single economic space; this process culminated on 8 July 1994 in the formal creation of the Central Asian Union (later the Central Asian Economic Community), with a council of heads of state, an executive committee and a Central Asian Bank. The exact dates of these steps vary somewhat across sources, so a general period rather than a single day is given here (Source: Wikipedia — Central Asian Union; Silk Road Studies — Central Asian integration).

Note: the bilateral 'Treaty of Eternal Friendship' between Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan was signed later — outside this window, in 1998 — so it is not included in this period's article (Source: Wikipedia — Kazakhstan–Uzbekistan relations).

Shared factors that shaped relations

Several shared features bind the two states: both are Turkic-majority, of Muslim tradition and former Soviet republics, and they share a long land border. Water became a structural element of relations: the Syr Darya flows from the upstream (Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan) through to Kazakhstan, tying the three states into a single water-and-energy dependency; both also border the shrinking Aral Sea. These water, transport and enclave issues set the tone of early relations (Source: Wikipedia — Foreign relations of Uzbekistan; RFE/RL — Central Asia: 30 years of independence).

The leaders: Karimov and Nazarbayev

In 1991–1996 the two states were led by the central political figures of the era. In Uzbekistan — Islam Karimov (from 1990, and after independence, until 2016); in Kazakhstan — Nursultan Nazarbayev, elected the first president of independent Kazakhstan on 1 December 1991 and leading until 2019. The two leaders met face to face several times in these years within CIS and Central Asian integration summits; however, no separate bilateral summit with a precise date in this very period could be confirmed to a reliable second source, so the meetings are mentioned in general terms (Source: Britannica — Islam Karimov; Wikipedia — Nursultan Nazarbayev; RFE/RL).

The capital context

In this period Kazakhstan's capital was Alma-Ata (Almaty). The decree to move the capital to Akmola was issued on 20 October 1997, and the city was renamed Astana on 6 May 1998. So throughout 1991–1996 the Kazakh capital that conducted dialogue with Uzbekistan was Alma-Ata (Source: Wikipedia — Capital relocation in Kazakhstan; Wikipedia — History of Astana).

Why this matters

Relations between Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan are among the most important bilateral ties in Central Asia. Their 1991–1996 foundation — the CIS, diplomatic relations and attempts at regional integration — set the course of cooperation in the following decades. This article presents only verified dates and facts; dates that differ across sources are marked as approximate.
