Uzbekistan's 1996 currency crisis and the restriction of som convertibility (1996–2003)

After the som was introduced in 1994, a poor 1996 cotton harvest and dwindling reserves caused a balance-of-payments crisis. That year Uzbekistan restricted foreign-exchange trading, halted som convertibility, and its IMF program broke down. The result was a multiple exchange-rate system that lasted years, with a large gap between the official and black-market rates. Only on 15 October 2003 did Uzbekistan accept IMF Article VIII obligations — current-account convertibility. This history is facts only.

19 November 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

One of the longest-running problems in independent Uzbekistan's economic history — the som's lack of free convertibility — began in 1996 and lasted nearly seven years. The following sets out this currency crisis and the convertibility restrictions on verified facts only; points where sources differ or that could not be confirmed are flagged.

Introduction of the som

Uzbekistan introduced a transitional 'som-coupon' in November 1993, and the full som on 1 July 1994 — 1 som equal to 1,000 som-coupons, at an initial rate of about 25 som to the US dollar. The country had chosen a gradualist reform path that liberalized prices and trade slowly and kept strong state control, rather than 'shock therapy.'

1996: the cotton shock and a balance-of-payments crisis

Cotton was Uzbekistan's main export and source of foreign exchange — about 40% of hard-currency earnings. In 1996 a poor harvest and low hard-currency reserves produced a balance-of-payments crisis. (Sources are not consistent on a sharp fall in the world cotton price in 1996 itself; the reliable factor for 1996 is the weak harvest and the shortage of reserves, while the world-price slump belongs more to 1997–1999.)

The policy response: convertibility restrictions

In late 1996 Uzbekistan reversed its earlier liberalization: it imposed measures restricting imports and foreign-currency purchases, convertibility restrictions, foreign-exchange surrender requirements, and import controls — tightening state control over the som. The relationship with the IMF broke down around this time: per secondary sources the standby arrangement was suspended in October 1996 — chiefly over the convertibility restrictions — and the IMF halted lending.

The multiple-rate system and the black market

The multiple-rate regime effectively began in September 1996. The government kept, side by side, two official (overvalued) rates — the Central Bank's official rate and a 'privileged' interbank rate for state enterprises and licensed importers — and a much weaker black-market rate. At times the black-market rate was about 4 times the official rate. After the May 2000 devaluation, for instance, the official rate was about 280 som to the dollar while the commercial rate was around 675. (The exact figures vary by date and by which 'official' rate — Central Bank or interbank — is quoted.)

Consequences

Suppressed convertibility, and the allocation of scarce hard currency through opaque processes favoring connected firms, hurt importers, foreign investors and ordinary traders; it fed shuttle trade and the informal economy as well as a thriving currency black market; and the som depreciated. At the same time, through its gradualist, state-controlled path, Uzbekistan avoided the sharp output collapse many post-Soviet states suffered — the standard scholarly assessment.

2003: IMF Article VIII

Uzbekistan accepted the obligations of IMF Article VIII (Sections 2(a), 3 and 4) with effect from 15 October 2003 — making the som convertible for current-account transactions (the IMF's formal announcement is dated 11 November 2003). Article VIII requires member states not to impose restrictions on payments and transfers for current international transactions, and not to engage in discriminatory currency arrangements or multiple currency practices without IMF approval. It covers current-account convertibility — not capital transfers (such as buying foreign stocks).

The longer legacy

2003 restored current-account convertibility, but capital controls remained and a de-facto gap persisted for years. Full liberalization and a free float came much later — in September 2017, under President Shavkat Mirziyoyev: the Central Bank unified the rates from 5 September 2017 and let the som float; the official rate jumped from about 4,200 to about 8,100 som to the dollar, converging with the black-market rate (roughly a 50% devaluation). This guide states only verified dates and facts.
