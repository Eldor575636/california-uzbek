US assistance programs explained: how SNAP, WIC and TANF work

The three main federal programs for low-income families — SNAP (food assistance), WIC (nutrition for women, infants and children) and TANF (temporary cash assistance) — explained: what each is, who can apply, and the immigrant rules (the five-year bar and the 2026 change to 'public charge').

24 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

The United States has several federal programs that help low-income families. Three of them are the most widely used: SNAP (food assistance), WIC (nutrition for pregnant women, infants and young children) and TANF (temporary cash assistance). This guide explains what each one is, who can apply, and which rules apply to immigrants — using verified facts only. The programs are set at the federal level but are often administered by the states, so exact conditions can vary from state to state (Source: USDA Food and Nutrition Service; Benefits.gov).

SNAP — food assistance (formerly 'food stamps')

SNAP (the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) is the largest food-assistance program in the US; it was formerly called 'food stamps.' At the federal level it is overseen by the US Department of Agriculture's Food and Nutrition Service (USDA FNS), but it is administered by the states — you apply at a state or local SNAP office. Benefits are loaded each month onto an EBT (Electronic Benefit Transfer) card, used like a debit card at USDA-authorized stores and supermarkets. The main requirement is that a household's gross monthly income be at or below 130% of the federal poverty level (and net income, after deductions, at or below 100%). SNAP can buy bread, fruits and vegetables, meat, fish, dairy, and seeds or plants that produce food; it cannot buy alcohol, tobacco, vitamins and medicines, hot prepared foods, or non-food household items. In fiscal year 2025 about 42 million Americans per month (roughly 12% of the population) used the program; the average benefit was about $185–190 per person per month (Source: USDA FNS; USDA Economic Research Service; Congressional Research Service R42505).

WIC — for women, infants and children

WIC's full name is the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children. It too is funded by USDA FNS and delivered locally by state WIC agencies. The program serves low-income pregnant, postpartum and breastfeeding women, infants, and children up to age 5 who meet income limits and are at nutritional risk. WIC provides specific food packages (milk, bread, fruit and more), nutrition education, breastfeeding support, and referrals to health and other services. In fiscal year 2024 an average of about 6.7 million people used it each month; WIC reaches an estimated 41% of all infants in the US (Source: USDA FNS — WIC; USDA Economic Research Service; CBPP).

TANF — temporary cash assistance

TANF's full name is Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. It provides cash assistance to low-income families, and that assistance is usually tied to work requirements. It is funded as a block grant by the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) — specifically its Administration for Children and Families (ACF); each state designs its own program within the grant, so benefit levels and rules vary sharply by state. TANF was created by the 1996 welfare-reform law (PRWORA) and replaced the earlier AFDC program. Under federal rules, a family that includes an adult can receive TANF cash assistance for no more than 60 months (five years) over a lifetime; states may exempt up to 20% of the caseload for hardship or set shorter limits (Source: CBPP; Congressional Research Service R44668).

Rules for immigrants

Most of these programs are open only to 'qualified immigrants.' Many lawful permanent residents (green-card holders) must wait five years before they can get SNAP, TANF, Medicaid, CHIP or SSI (the 'five-year bar'). Exempt from this wait are: refugees, asylees, people granted withholding of removal, certain trafficking survivors, Amerasians, and Iraqi/Afghan Special Immigrant Visa (SIV) holders. For SNAP specifically, green-card children under 18, recipients of a disability benefit, and those with 40 qualifying work quarters are also eligible without the wait. Undocumented immigrants are not eligible for SNAP, TANF or non-emergency Medicaid. WIC does not require 'qualified immigrant' status. Importantly: a child who is a US citizen or green-card holder can receive SNAP, Medicaid, CHIP and WIC even if the parents are ineligible — eligibility is determined per person, and a parent can apply on the child's behalf without disclosing their own immigration status or SSN (Source: National Immigration Law Center; USDA FNS — SNAP noncitizen eligibility).

The 'public charge' rule (2026 update)

'Public charge' is an assessment applied to certain green-card or visa applicants that weighs whether a person is likely to become heavily dependent on government support in the future. This rule has changed over time. Under the 2022 rule, only cash assistance (such as TANF and SSI) and long-term government-funded institutional care were counted; SNAP, WIC and non-long-term Medicaid were not. A new DHS/USCIS rule that took effect on 18 September 2026 may consider any means-tested benefit — including Medicaid, CHIP, SNAP, WIC, TANF, SSI, public housing assistance and postsecondary financial aid — as one factor in the assessment. Benefits received before 18 September 2026 are not counted; only those received on or after that date may be weighed. Emergency Medicaid, Medicaid for children under 21, and pregnancy-related Medicaid are generally still not counted. Public charge does not apply to refugees or asylees; and a child's own benefit use is not attributed to the parent. Because the rules change, consult official sources (USCIS, USDA FNS) or a qualified attorney before applying (Source: USCIS — Public Charge alert; Federal Register 2026-14539; KFF).

Where to apply

SNAP and TANF applications go through your state's human/social services office — in most states online, by phone, or in person. For WIC, contact a local WIC clinic. The federal Benefits.gov portal can help you make a preliminary check of what you may qualify for, but the final decision is made by the state office. Applying is free; be wary of 'middlemen' who demand money to get benefits for you (Source: Benefits.gov; USDA FNS).

Why this matters

Knowing how these programs work helps newcomer families understand their rights: which support is for food, which is cash, who can apply, and how immigration status affects things. This article presents only verified rules and figures; it offers no judgments or interpretation. Exact conditions can change by state and over time, so rely on official sources for the most current information.
