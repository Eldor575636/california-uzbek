The four Sunni schools of Islamic law: Hanafi, Shafi'i, Maliki and Hanbali — where they differ

Sunni Islam has four main schools of jurisprudence (madhhabs): Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali. They agree on creed and fundamentals and differ mainly on secondary (furu') rulings. Uzbekistan and Central Asia follow predominantly the Hanafi school.

25 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

In Sunni Islam, fiqh — the science of deriving practical religious rulings from the Qur'an and Sunnah — has historically been organized into four main schools, or madhhabs: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali. The word 'madhhab' means 'way' or 'path' in Arabic, and each madhhab is a consistent, internally coherent methodology for deriving rulings from the sources. These four schools recognize one another's validity and have taken part in scholarly debate with each other for centuries (Source: Wikipedia — Madhhab; Wikipedia — Fiqh).

What a madhhab is and how fiqh is structured

The science of fiqh is traditionally divided into two parts: usul al-fiqh — the roots and methods of deriving rulings (the rules for working with evidence such as the Qur'an, Sunnah, consensus and analogy) — and furu' al-fiqh, the practical rulings that result from applying those methods to specific questions. The four schools hold the same position on creed and the main pillars of the religion; the differences between them relate mainly to the secondary (furu') details of worship and transactions, and are generally minor (Source: Wikipedia — Fiqh; Wikipedia — Madhhab).

The four imams and their era

The four schools take their names from four great imams who lived in the 8th and 9th centuries. Imam Abu Hanifa (al-Nu'man ibn Thabit, c. 699–767 CE / 80–150 AH) is considered the founder of the Hanafi school. Imam Malik ibn Anas (c. 711–795 CE / 93–179 AH) — the Maliki school. Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 CE / 150–204 AH) — the Shafi'i school. Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855 CE / 164–241 AH) — the Hanbali school. Notably, al-Shafi'i studied fiqh for a time in Medina under Imam Malik (Source: Britannica — Abu Hanifah; Wikipedia — Malik ibn Anas; Wikipedia — Al-Shafi'i; Britannica — Ahmad ibn Hanbal).

The Hanafi school

The Hanafi school is attributed to Imam Abu Hanifa. He was born and raised in the city of Kufa in Iraq and was educated there; according to the sources he died in Baghdad in 767 CE (150 AH). The Hanafi school is known for giving wide scope to rational reasoning and analogy alongside textual evidence. Today it is the most widely followed school by number of adherents, and is predominant especially in Central and South Asia and in Turkey (Source: Britannica — Abu Hanifah; Wikipedia — Hanafi school; Wikipedia — Madhhab).

The Maliki school

The founder of the Maliki school, Imam Malik ibn Anas, was born in Medina and spent most of his life there. His famous work, al-Muwatta', collects the practical religious norms of the people of Medina. A distinctive feature of the Maliki school is that it treats the practice of the people of Medina (ahl al-amal) as an important source in understanding the Sunnah. This school is today predominant mainly in North and West Africa (Source: Wikipedia — Malik ibn Anas; Wikipedia — Maliki school).

The Shafi'i school

The Shafi'i school is attributed to Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 CE). He died in the city of Fustat in Egypt. Al-Shafi'i played a formative role in systematizing usul al-fiqh — the science of the foundations of jurisprudence — and is recognized in many sources as its founder; his work al-Risala is a key text in that field. The Shafi'i school places particular emphasis on hadith in deriving rulings and is today widespread mainly in East Africa and Southeast Asia (Source: Wikipedia — Al-Shafi'i; Wikipedia — Al-Risala; Wikipedia — Madhhab).

The Hanbali school

The Hanbali school is attributed to Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855 CE). He was born in Baghdad and died there. Ahmad ibn Hanbal compiled a large hadith collection called al-Musnad. The Hanbali school is considered the most text-bound (traditionalist) of the four schools: it relies chiefly on the Qur'an and Sunnah. Today this school is predominant mainly in the north and center of the Arabian Peninsula — including Saudi Arabia (Source: Britannica — Ahmad ibn Hanbal; Wikipedia — Ahmad ibn Hanbal; Wikipedia — Madhhab).

Usul and furu': what they agree on, what they differ on

The four schools are of one mind on the foundations of the religion — such as the authority of the Qur'an and Sunnah, the pillars of faith, and the obligation of worship. The differences relate mainly to furu' — the details of secondary, practical rulings: for example, the order of certain acts of worship or the fine points of transactions. For this reason the four schools view one another not as invalid but as mutually recognized paths within Sunni fiqh (Source: Wikipedia — Madhhab; Wikipedia — Fiqh).

Uzbekistan and Central Asia

Uzbekistan and Central Asia follow predominantly the Hanafi school, both historically and today. The distinctive character of the region's Islam has been shaped, alongside Hanafi fiqh, by the Maturidi school of theology, named after the Samarkand-born theologian Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (died c. 944, Samarkand). The region also contributed greatly to the science of hadith: Sahih al-Bukhari, compiled by Imam Muhammad al-Bukhari of Bukhara (c. 810–870), is regarded as one of the most authoritative hadith collections in Islamic scholarship (Source: Britannica — Abu Mansur Muhammad al-Maturidi; Silk Road Studies — Religion and the Secular State in Uzbekistan; Wikipedia — Madhhab).

Why this matters

Knowing the structure of the madhhabs helps make sense of diversity within the religion: it explains why Muslims agree on fundamentals yet may differ in some practical matters, and where Uzbekistan stands within that tradition. This article presents only verified dates and evidence; it issues no fatwa or judgment on which madhhab is 'correct' or on any religious ruling.
