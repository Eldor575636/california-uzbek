Emergency medical help in the USA: how 911, ambulances, the ER and urgent care work

In the US, calling 911 in an emergency is free from any phone, and by law the Emergency Room (ER) must screen and stabilize anyone — regardless of citizenship or ability to pay (EMTALA, 1986). But the ambulance is not free and an uninsured ER visit is expensive. This facts-only guide explains 911 and 988, the difference between the ER and urgent care, the costs, and practical rules for immigrants.

19 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

For many Uzbekistanis new to America the medical system feels complex: when do you call 911, where do you go — the ER or urgent care, how much does an ambulance cost, and what happens if you have no insurance? The answers below rely only on official sources (FCC, CMS, HHS, IRS, NIH). This is information, not medical advice.

911 — the emergency number

911 is the single US emergency number: the call reaches a dispatcher for police, fire or emergency medical services (EMS). It is free from any phone. Under FCC rules, wireless carriers must put through a 911 call even from a phone with no SIM card or one that was never activated. Use 911 only when life or limb is threatened — chest pain, trouble breathing, severe bleeding, loss of consciousness, stroke or seizure signs, poisoning, serious injury. Text-to-911 is available in many areas; the FCC's advice: 'Call if you can; text if you can't.'

988 and the poison line

For a mental-health crisis or suicidal thoughts, call or text 988 — the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is free, confidential, 24/7 and offers interpretation in over 240 languages. For poisoning (medicine, household chemicals) the national Poison Help line is 1-800-222-1222, free and 24/7. It is wise to save these numbers in your phone in advance.

EMTALA: emergency care for everyone

Under EMTALA (the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act, 1986), any hospital that participates in Medicare and has an emergency department must give a medical screening exam to anyone who comes in, and — if an emergency condition (including active labor) is found — provide stabilizing treatment. As CMS states, this applies 'regardless of ability to pay,' and regardless of citizenship or immigration status. Important caveat: EMTALA guarantees you will be examined and stabilized, but it does not make the care free — you can still be billed.

ER vs urgent care vs primary care

The Emergency Room (ER) is open 24/7 and handles life-threatening conditions (chest pain, stroke signs, severe bleeding, major trauma, poisoning); waits are usually long and costs highest. Urgent care handles non-life-threatening but can't-wait problems (colds, flu, earache, minor cuts and sprains); waits are shorter and costs much lower. For routine or ongoing problems, prescriptions and advice, primary care or telehealth is the cheapest route. Per NIH, the same care in the ER can cost 2–3 times or more than in a doctor's office.

The ambulance is not free

An ambulance is billed separately. A ground ambulance typically costs from several hundred to several thousand dollars (a base fee plus a per-mile charge); an air ambulance can cost far more — the exact amount varies by situation and state. Ambulances are often out-of-network, creating surprise bills. The No Surprises Act (in effect since 2022) bans surprise bills for most emergency services and for out-of-network AIR ambulances; however, GROUND ambulances were carved out of that law — some states add their own protections.

Costs without insurance, and reducing the bill

Even uninsured, you are treated first and billed afterward. Nonprofit hospitals, under IRS Section 501(r), must have a written Financial Assistance Policy, must not demand payment before emergency care, and cannot charge assistance-eligible patients more than the amount generally billed to insured patients. Practical steps: ask the hospital for its financial-assistance (charity care) application, request an itemized bill, and ask for a discount or a payment plan.

Practical rules for immigrants

In a true emergency you will be treated regardless of immigration status (EMTALA). Carry a photo ID and your insurance card (if any) — they do not block treatment but speed up registration. Hospitals that receive federal funds (Medicare/Medicaid) must provide a free qualified interpreter under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act — you are not charged for it and are not required to use a relative or friend to interpret. Emergency Medicaid covers only the emergency itself for people who meet Medicaid income rules but are ineligible for full Medicaid because of immigration status — not ongoing care after you are stabilized.

Key numbers

911 — police, fire, emergency medical (free from any phone). 988 — Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call/text/chat, 24/7, multilingual). 1-800-222-1222 — national Poison Help line, free and 24/7.

Why this matters

In an emergency, a fast, correct decision — which number to call, where to go, and what rights you have — saves both health and money. For diaspora families the key fact is simple: in a true emergency, care does not depend on your immigration status, but it is not free — so after you are treated, ask about financial assistance and payment plans. This guide states only verified facts and is not a substitute for medical or legal advice.
