Primary, secondary and anonymous sources: how to judge a news report

In a stream of news, not every source carries the same weight. This guide explains the difference between primary and secondary sources, and between named and anonymous sources, strictly on the facts. The aim is to help the reader see what a report actually rests on. All definitions draw on academic libraries and journalism codes of ethics.

20 November 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

Introduction: not every source is equal

A single news report can rest on many sources, but those sources differ in reliability and in how much they prove. There is a real gap between an original document about an event and an article that retells it. In the same way, a source whose name is disclosed is not the same as a source whose name is withheld. This guide explains two basic distinctions: primary versus secondary sources, and named versus anonymous sources. Every explanation rests only on facts and favors no side.

Primary source

A primary source is an original, first-hand record or piece of evidence created at the time of an event or by a direct participant. It is the raw material for study and analysis. Familiar examples for readers: laws, court rulings and filings, treaties, government reports, a company's own financial filing or press release, datasets and statistics in their original form, the full transcript of a speech or hearing as well as its complete audio or video, raw unedited footage and photographs, eyewitness testimony, and a scientific study as published by its own authors.

Secondary source

A secondary source describes, summarizes, analyzes or interprets primary sources. It is usually created after the fact and by someone who was not a participant. Examples: a news article reporting on a ruling, a study or a speech; opinion columns and editorials; reviews; textbooks and reference works; documentaries and explainer pieces. An important nuance: the same document can be primary or secondary depending on how it is used. A news article is a secondary source about the event, but if you are studying how the media covered that event, the same article becomes a primary source.

Why this distinction matters

Tracing a claim back to its primary source is the foundation of judging a report correctly. Secondary accounts can simplify information, omit important context, or misstate it. A headline or a short report may compress a nuanced study into an inaccurate single claim. For that reason, whenever possible it is best to read the original: the full study, the actual document, and the complete transcript.

Lateral reading and the SIFT method

Fact-checkers do not judge a page only by reading it top to bottom, known as vertical reading. They leave the page, open other tabs, and see what other reputable sources say about the claim and about the outlet; this is called lateral reading. The SIFT method has four steps: Stop (pause and check the claim against yourself); Investigate the source; Find better or other coverage; and Trace claims, quotes and media back to their original context.

Named versus anonymous sources

Reputable newsrooms treat a named source as far stronger than an unnamed one, because a person whose name is public is accountable; anonymity is treated as an exception. The ground rules set by the Associated Press and documented by Poynter are as follows: on the record means the material is usable and the source is named; off the record means the material cannot be published at all; on background means it is publishable but under negotiated conditions, usually describing rather than naming the source, for example a senior official; deep background means usable but with no attribution of any kind; and not for attribution means publishable but the name cannot be used, which is distinct from off the record, where the material itself cannot be used.

Conditions placed on an anonymous source

The Associated Press permits anonymous material only under these conditions: first, it must be information rather than opinion or speculation, and it must be vital to the report; second, the information must not be available except on the condition of anonymity; and third, the source must be reliable and in a position to have accurate information. In addition, editor approval is required, and opinion or speculation from anonymous sources is barred. According to the Reuters handbook, a named source is always preferable and anonymous sources are the weakest sources; a single anonymous source is used only in exceptional cases and with particularly rigorous cross-checking. The Society of Professional Journalists (SPJ) Code of Ethics requires journalists to identify sources clearly, reserves anonymity for sources who may face danger or retribution and who hold information unavailable elsewhere, and asks that the reason anonymity was granted be explained.

How to read the phrase a person close to the matter

A phrase such as a person close to the matter signals that the outlet is vouching for someone it will not name. When reading such a phrase it helps to ask: does the outlet explain why the source is anonymous; how close is the source to the event, that is, direct knowledge versus hearsay; is the claim corroborated by a named source or a document; and is this a single anonymous source or several independent ones. Opinion attributed to an anonymous source is a red flag.

A practical checklist for the reader

First, identify who the original source is and trace it; do not stop at the outlet that merely repeated it. Second, is the source named; if anonymous, is a reason given and is the source described well enough to judge its access. Third, can you find the primary document, that is the study, ruling, data or transcript. Fourth, how many independent sources confirm it; a single-source report is weaker than a multi-source one, and independent confirmation beats repetition across outlets copying one another. Fifth, cross-check and read laterally. Sixth, watch for distortion: opinion dressed up as fact, a headline stronger than the body, and numbers stripped of their context.
