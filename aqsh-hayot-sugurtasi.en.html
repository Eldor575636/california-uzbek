How Life Insurance Works in the USA: A Guide for Immigrants

Life insurance is a contract with an insurer that pays a named beneficiary when the insured person dies. Here are its core concepts, types, cost factors, and US tax rules.

28 October 2026 · 4 min read · California Uzbek

Life insurance is a contract between a person and an insurance company. If the insured person dies, the company pays a death benefit to a pre-named beneficiary. In exchange, the policy owner pays a regular premium. The contract separates three roles: the owner (who controls the policy and pays), the insured (whose life is covered), and the beneficiary (who receives the money). These roles can be the same person or different people (Source: Insurance Information Institute).

The difference between term and permanent insurance

There are two main types. Term life covers a set period — typically 10, 20, or 30 years — and pays out only if death occurs during that period. When the term ends, coverage ends. Term life builds no cash value and is therefore cheaper.

Permanent life — including whole life and universal life — stays in force for the insured's whole life as long as premiums are paid. Its premium is higher because part of the payment builds cash value. Cash value accumulates over time and can be borrowed against or withdrawn, but any unpaid loan reduces the eventual death benefit (Source: Insurance Information Institute).

How underwriting works

Price and acceptance are determined during underwriting: an application is filled out, usually a medical exam and lab tests are taken, and the applicant is placed in a risk class. Some companies offer accelerated underwriting, which uses prescription history, driving records, and MIB data to decide without an exam, often within about 48 hours (Source: NAIC).

For those who prefer no exam, there are no-exam and guaranteed-issue options. Guaranteed-issue policies are issued without health questions or an exam, but their coverage is limited — often around $25,000–$30,000 — and relatively more expensive (Source: NAIC, Insurance Information Institute).

How much coverage is typically considered

There are two common ways to size coverage. The first is an income-replacement multiple — a set multiple of annual income (some advisors cite 10–20 times pre-tax salary). The second is the DIME method: summing Debt, Income, Mortgage, and Education costs to estimate a family's need. Funeral and administrative costs are also added to the calculation (Source: Insurance Information Institute).

There are two ways to obtain coverage: group life through an employer, and a standalone individual policy. Group coverage is often cheap or free but is usually capped and may be lost when leaving the job; an individual policy belongs to the owner and stays regardless of employment.

Factors that affect cost

The premium depends on several factors: age, health status, smoking (nicotine use), the chosen term length, and the coverage amount. Price rises sharply with age — the same coverage can cost a 60-year-old several times more than a 30-year-old. Smokers pay roughly 2–4 times more than non-smokers for the same policy. A longer term also costs more because the rate is guaranteed for more years (Source: Insurance Information Institute).

Tax rules in the USA

According to the US Internal Revenue Service (IRS), a death benefit received by a beneficiary due to the insured's death is generally not included in gross income and is free of income tax. Exception: if interest is added to the payout, that interest is taxable and must be reported as income. Cash-value growth in permanent policies accumulates on a tax-deferred basis (Source: IRS).

Buying safely and avoiding scams

Most states grant a free-look period on a new policy — often 10 days, sometimes up to 30 — during which the policy can be canceled for a refund. If premiums go unpaid, the policy can lapse and coverage is lost. To avoid mis-selling and fraud, it is advised to verify that the company is licensed by the state insurance regulator and to read the written document for policy terms, fees, and exclusions (Source: NAIC).

Takaful — an alternative for Muslims

Conventional, interest-based life insurance raises religious questions for some Muslims regarding riba (interest), gharar (excessive uncertainty), and maysir (gambling), because companies may invest pooled funds in interest-bearing instruments. As an alternative, takaful — Islamic cooperative (mutual-aid) insurance — exists. In takaful, participants contribute to a shared risk pool as tabarru' (donation), and losses are shared among members (Source: Society of Actuaries; Institute of Islamic Banking and Insurance).
