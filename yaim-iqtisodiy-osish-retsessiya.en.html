What GDP, economic growth and recession are: a full reference

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the headline figure that measures the size of a country's economy. This reference explains what GDP is, the difference between nominal and real GDP, how economic growth is measured, what a recession is, and how these concepts apply to Uzbekistan's figures, on the basis of sources.

18 October 2026 · 3 min read · California Uzbek

The figure that appears most often in economic news is gross domestic product (GDP). According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF), GDP measures the monetary value of final goods and services produced in a country in a given period (a quarter or a year). The word "final" matters: to avoid double-counting, only finished products are counted, not intermediate goods (for example the flour used to make bread).

Nominal and real GDP

Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, with no adjustment for inflation. Real GDP is adjusted for price changes (inflation or deflation) by using a price deflator to convert values to constant prices. That is why real GDP is used to compare the economy's true growth from year to year — a rise in the nominal figure often simply reflects higher prices. (Source: IMF.)

Economic growth and GDP per capita

Economic growth is the percentage change in real GDP from one period to the next (usually the annual growth rate). GDP per capita is total GDP divided by the population; it is average output per person and is used as a rough proxy for average income. (Source: World Bank, IMF.)

How GDP is calculated

Under the expenditure approach, GDP is the sum of four components: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Here C is household consumption, I is business investment, G is government spending on goods and services, X is exports and M is imports; (X − M) is net exports. (Source: U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, BEA, 2025.)

GDP versus GNI

GDP measures output produced within a country's borders. Gross national income (GNI) adds to GDP the net income (wages, property income and so on) that residents receive from abroad. For this reason remittances sent home by citizens working abroad count toward GNI, not GDP — an important distinction for countries such as Uzbekistan where migrant remittances are large. The World Bank classifies economies as low-, middle- or high-income precisely by GNI per capita. (Source: OECD, World Bank.)

What a recession is

In the media a recession is often defined as "two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP" — but that is a rule of thumb (a "technical recession"), not the official definition. In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which dates the start and end of recessions, defines one as "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months," weighing depth, diffusion and duration together. According to the NBER, the 2001 recession did not include two consecutive quarters of decline, while the first half of 2022 had two negative quarters but was not declared a recession. (Source: NBER; U.S. Congressional Research Service, CRS.)

Uzbekistan in figures

According to IMF estimates, Uzbekistan's real GDP grew by 6.3% in 2023; for 2024 the IMF cites about 6.5% (the national statistics agency reported a higher figure, around 7.4%). For 2025, World Bank data and the IMF's April 2026 statement show growth of 7.7%; for 2026 the IMF projects 6.8% growth. (Source: IMF; World Bank.)

According to World Bank data, Uzbekistan's nominal GDP was about US$115 billion in 2024 and around US$147 billion in 2025; GDP per capita reached roughly US$3,968 in 2025. The country's population was about 37 million as of 2025. For comparison, the World Bank puts world GDP at about US$111.7 trillion in current prices in 2024. Sources can differ on individual figures depending on the year and methodology, so a single source and year is given for each figure. (Source: World Bank, IMF.)

Conclusion

GDP is the main tool for tracking the size and growth of an economy, but it does not fully measure well-being: distribution (inequality), the non-monetary economy, environmental damage and income from abroad (remittances) are not fully reflected in it. When reading economic news, telling nominal from real figures, reading the growth rate, and noting the source and year are the keys to understanding the numbers correctly.
