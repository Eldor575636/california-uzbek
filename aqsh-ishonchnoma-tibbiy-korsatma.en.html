How a power of attorney and an advance healthcare directive work in the United States

A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document that gives one person authority to make decisions about your money, property or affairs. An advance directive sets out your treatment wishes, or names a decision-maker, for when you can no longer decide for yourself. The rules vary from state to state in the US.

26 November 2026 · 5 min read · California Uzbek

A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document in which one person gives another authority to make decisions about their money, property or legal affairs. The person granting the authority is called the 'principal'; the person receiving it is called the 'agent', or 'attorney-in-fact' — a title that does not mean the agent is a lawyer. In the US a POA is governed not by federal law but by state law, and the requirements vary from state to state (Source: CFPB — Managing someone else's money: agents guide; American Bar Association — Power of attorney).

Types of POA: general and limited

By the scope of authority, a POA falls into two main types. A general POA gives the agent broad authority over financial and legal matters. A limited or special POA restricts the authority to a particular task or period — for example, only to complete a single real-estate transaction. A POA covering money and property (bank accounts, bills, investments, real estate) is called a financial POA (Source: Nolo — Power of attorney; American Bar Association — Power of attorney).

'Durable' and 'springing' POA

The key distinction is whether the POA remains in effect after the principal loses mental capacity (for example, through serious illness or loss of consciousness). A 'durable' POA continues to operate after the principal becomes incapacitated; a non-durable POA ends automatically in exactly that situation. A 'springing' POA takes effect not at signing but only when a specified later event occurs — typically the principal's incapacity (Source: American Bar Association — Power of attorney; Cornell Legal Information Institute — Springing durable power of attorney).

The agent as a fiduciary

The agent is legally a 'fiduciary' — a trusted representative obliged to manage the principal's money and property for the principal's benefit, not the agent's own. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) lists four main duties: act only in the principal's interest; manage the money and property carefully; keep the principal's funds separate from the agent's own; and keep good records. Breaching these duties can lead to removal from the role, a lawsuit, or even criminal liability (Source: CFPB — Managing someone else's money: agents guide; CFPB — What is a fiduciary).

When a POA ends

While the principal still has capacity, they may revoke the POA at any time; the agent then stops acting, and anyone holding a copy should be notified. A POA ends automatically when the principal dies — at that point the agent loses all authority, even to pay remaining bills. If the agent is the principal's spouse, the authority may end on divorce; it may also end if a court appoints a guardian or conservator for the principal. It should be remembered that a POA is not a will: a POA operates during the principal's lifetime and ends at death, whereas a will does the opposite and takes effect after death (Source: CFPB — Managing someone else's money: agents guide; Nolo — Power of attorney).

Medical POA and advance directive

In health care there are two main documents. A 'living will' is a document in which a person records their wishes about medical treatment — especially life-sustaining treatment near the end of life — for when they can no longer speak for themselves; it only gives instructions and does not name a decision-maker. A medical POA (a healthcare proxy, or durable power of attorney for health care) names a trusted person to make medical decisions on the patient's behalf when the patient can no longer communicate. The two documents are used together; many states combine them into a single 'advance directive' (Source: National Institute on Aging — Advance care planning: advance directives for health care).

An advance directive is a legal document, not a medical order. Medical orders signed by a physician — such as a POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment) or a DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) — are separate documents that emergency services and doctors must follow. A DNR concerns only cardiopulmonary resuscitation; a POLST covers a wider range of treatments and is usually intended for seriously or chronically ill patients (Source: National POLST — POLST and advance directives; National Institute on Aging — Advance care planning).

Practical points

In most states a lawyer is not legally required to complete these documents, but a notary's certification and/or witnesses are often required — the exact requirement depends on each state's law. Many states publish free template forms. Once the documents are prepared, copies should be given to the healthcare proxy, the family doctor and close relatives, while the original is kept in a safe place. In the US, advance care planning discussions are covered by Medicare as part of the annual wellness visit (Source: CFPB — Managing someone else's money; National Institute on Aging — Advance care planning).

Using these documents abroad: for immigrants

A POA or advance directive drawn up in the US may not be recognized automatically in another state or abroad — the rules vary by jurisdiction. To use a US document in a country that is a member of the Hague Apostille Convention, it must be certified with an apostille issued by the relevant authority (the US Department of State for federal documents, the state authority for state-level documents) and is usually required to be officially translated into the language of the destination country. Uzbekistan is a member of the Hague Apostille Convention (which entered into force for Uzbekistan on 15 April 2012), so a properly apostilled US document is recognized there — but with some states there may be additional limitations, so the specific situation should be checked with a consulate or lawyer (Source: U.S. Department of State — Authenticate your document for use abroad; HCCH — Apostille Convention status).

Why this matters

A power of attorney and an advance directive allow immigrant families to decide in advance, for emergencies — illness, surgery or loss of capacity — who will manage money, property and medical decisions, and how. This article gives general information and is not a substitute for legal advice; for a specific document and state requirements, it is advisable to consult the state bar association or a licensed lawyer.
