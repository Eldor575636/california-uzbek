Qard hasan: how a benevolent, interest-free loan works in Islam

Qard hasan — Arabic for "a beautiful loan" — is an interest-free, benevolent loan given to someone in need in Islam. The borrower repays only the principal received; no interest or markup is taken, because that would be riba. The Qur'an mentions the concept in several verses (for example al-Baqara 2:245, al-Hadid 57:11, al-Taghabun 64:17). Qard hasan differs from charity: charity is not repaid, but a loan is. Although interest (riba) is forbidden in Islamic finance, one Muslim lending money to another is not only permitted but considered a rewarded act. Its classical form is qard hasan. The following explains what qard hasan is, which sources it rests on, and how it differs from charity — facts only.

What qard hasan is

Qard hasan is Arabic for a "beautiful" or "benevolent loan." It is an interest-free loan given to a person in need, where the borrower repays only the principal received. The lender demands no interest, markup or profit share — otherwise it becomes riba and is forbidden in Islam. In this, qard hasan differs fundamentally from a modern interest-bearing loan, where the debtor pays interest on top of the principal.

The Qur'anic basis

The phrase "qard hasan" appears several times in the Qur'an. Surah al-Baqara, verse 245, says: "Who is it that will lend to Allah a goodly loan so He may multiply it for him many times over?" Similar meaning appears in al-Hadid (57:11), al-Taghabun (64:17) and al-Muzzammil (73:20). In these verses, giving an interest-free loan to the needy is likened to a "beautiful loan" given to Allah, with a manifold reward promised.

A loan, not charity

Qard hasan differs from sadaqa (charity) and zakat. Charity and zakat are non-repayable giving; qard hasan is a loan to be repaid. That is, the borrower must return the sum received by the agreed time. This very feature helps a person get back on their feet while preserving the responsibility of a debt.

No markup, but real costs may be covered

In qard hasan, no markup tied to the amount or the term is taken. However, many scholars hold that a real, defined administrative cost of arranging the loan (such as a documentation or service fee) may be recovered — on condition that it is not calculated as a percentage of the loan and does not become hidden riba.

Collateral and a guarantor are allowed

In qard hasan the lender may ask for collateral (rahn) or a guarantor to secure repayment — this is not riba but a means of ensuring the loan returns. Writing the loan down is also recommended; al-Baqara verse 282 calls for recording a debt and bringing witnesses.

If the debtor is in hardship — grant time

Al-Baqara verse 280 advises, if the debtor is in financial hardship, to grant relief by postponing the repayment: "And if someone is in hardship, then let there be postponement until a time of ease." So in qard hasan the lender is expected to show patience and leniency, not to press someone in difficulty.

Where it appears in practice

Qard hasan appears in various forms in practice: mutual help within a family or neighborhood, benevolent funds held by a mosque or community, and interest-free loan products that some Islamic banks offer on a limited scale. In some countries qard hasan funds and cooperatives are set up to give interest-free loans to students, small entrepreneurs or those in emergency need.

Why this matters for Uzbekistan and the diaspora

Debt is a vital matter for many families, and interest-bearing credit raises questions from the standpoint of riba. Knowing the concept of qard hasan points to a religiously sound alternative based on mutual help. This reference explains the general religious-legal basis; for a specific case, consulting a qualified scholar or a trusted religious body is advised.
